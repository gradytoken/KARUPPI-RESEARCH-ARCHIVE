# PSYCH-001 — The Righteous Mind: Conditional KARUPPI Transfer Note

**Source study:** 02_BOOK_STUDIES/PSYCH_001_The_Righteous_Mind.md  
**Source:** Jonathan Haidt, *The Righteous Mind: Why Good People Are Divided by Politics and Religion* (Pantheon Books, 2012)  
**Status:** Research application only. No story canon changes approved.  
**Project boundary:** Preserve the present-day 1990 village mystery. The removed historical Story B mythology remains out of active development. Do not invent a new mythology, causal explanation, villain, cult or ritual purpose from this psychology framework.

## 1. Why this book matters to KARUPPI

The book's central value is its account of how ordinary people form moral judgments, explain their conduct, protect identities and bind themselves to groups. It gives the story team a method for writing human conflict without sorting characters into good and evil camps.

A resident may genuinely care for a neighbour and still participate in excluding another person. A family member may protect a relative because of loyalty, while another family member insists on fairness or truth. A leader may understand authority as a duty to protect the community, while a subordinate experiences the same order as domination. A ritual may create solidarity and emotional meaning for participants without every participant understanding it in the same way.

Those contradictions can produce suspense and emotional stakes without making villagers into caricatures.

## 2. The six foundations as a story diagnostic

Use the foundations as questions, not labels.

| Foundation | Ask of the character | What the conflict may look like |
|---|---|---|
| Care / harm | Whose suffering feels immediate and important to this person? Whose suffering do they overlook? | Protecting a vulnerable person versus obeying a rule |
| Fairness / cheating | What does this person believe someone deserves? What counts as cheating or free riding? | Equal treatment versus reward or punishment according to contribution |
| Loyalty / betrayal | Who belongs to this person's circle? What would count as betrayal? | Family loyalty versus friendship, truth or duty |
| Authority / subversion | Whose authority does this person accept, and what responsibility do they believe authority carries? | Respect for elders or leaders versus resistance to coercion |
| Sanctity / degradation | What is considered pure, untouchable, sacred or polluted? | Preserving a sacred boundary versus revealing a truth |
| Liberty / oppression | When does this person feel controlled, bullied or deprived of choice? | Collective protection versus personal freedom |

A character can be high on several concerns at once. Do not assign one foundation as a permanent identity or use Haidt's American political groupings to classify villagers.

## 3. Character worksheet: intuition, justification and social cost

Use this worksheet for each major character when their established story role is being developed. It is a research instrument. It does not authorise a new action or backstory.

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Immediate trigger | What precise event, image, sound, discovery or accusation produces the first reaction? |
| First intuition | What do they instinctively feel: fear, disgust, grief, anger, tenderness, shame, pride or relief? |
| Primary moral concern | Which foundation or combination is activated? |
| Private explanation | What do they tell themselves about why their response is justified? |
| Public explanation | What do they tell family, neighbours, elders or investigators? |
| Reputation at stake | Whose respect, trust or approval could they lose? |
| In-group boundary | Who counts as "one of us" to this character? Who is outside that circle? |
| Sacred value | What person, place, object, practice or principle must not be violated? |
| Expected sanction | What punishment, shame, gossip, exclusion or loss do they anticipate? |
| Personal cost | What do they sacrifice to remain consistent with their stated value? |
| Contradiction | Where does their actual behaviour violate the moral identity they claim? |
| Exception | For whom or under what condition would they break their own rule? |
| Evidence threshold | What would make them reconsider, and who would have to present it? |
| Change condition | What relationship, experience or consequence could change their intuition rather than merely defeat their argument? |

### The key test

Do not stop at "What does this character believe?"

Ask:

- What do they fear losing?
- What do they feel they owe?
- Who are they trying to protect?
- Who are they trying not to disappoint?
- What would make them feel ashamed?
- What action would they never admit to themselves?
- What new evidence would threaten their identity?

## 4. Conflict architecture

Haidt's ideas can be adapted into a practical sequence:

**Trigger → intuition → explanation → audience → group response → consequence**

1. **Trigger:** An event or discovery activates a moral concern.
2. **Intuition:** The character reacts before they have a complete explanation.
3. **Explanation:** They construct a reason that makes the reaction defensible.
4. **Audience:** They consider who must hear, approve, obey or remain silent.
5. **Group response:** Others protect, condemn, gossip, punish, excuse or reinterpret the action.
6. **Consequence:** The response creates a new injury, obligation, fear, resentment or deeper commitment.

The sequence helps identify where a character's public account differs from their private motivation. It should not be treated as a fixed formula for every scene.

## 6. Designing a morally divided community without caricature

A useful village is not a single mind. Even if residents share a broad moral matrix, they can disagree about which value should take priority in a specific situation.

Possible internal differences to test, only where the Master Story Bible supports the relevant characters and relationships:

- An elder may view the preservation of a rule as a duty to future generations.
- A parent may support the same rule until it threatens their child.
- A younger resident may accept the community's beliefs but reject the authority enforcing them.
- A neighbour may value fairness and resent a family that receives special treatment.
- A participant in a communal ritual may feel genuine solidarity while another experiences pressure or fear.
- A person may be generous to insiders but unable to extend the same compassion to an outsider.
- Someone may defend a leader publicly while privately recognising that the leader has failed their responsibilities.

These are character-pattern prompts, not proposals to assign these roles to existing named characters.

**Do not make belief itself the villain.** Study what people do with belief: care, cooperate, interpret, conceal, punish, protect, exploit or resist.

## 7. Emotional conflict combinations

Use these combinations to test whether a scene contains a real emotional dilemma:

| Collision | Emotional question |
|---|---|
| Love vs loyalty | Can I protect someone I love without betraying everyone else? |
| Duty vs conscience | What happens when obedience feels wrong? |
| Shame vs truth | Can I expose what happened if it destroys the identity I have lived by? |
| Fairness vs compassion | Should someone receive what they deserve, or what they need? |
| Belonging vs liberty | How much of myself must I surrender to remain accepted? |
| Sacredness vs curiosity | Is it wrong to investigate what everyone says must remain untouched? |
| Authority vs protection | What if the person responsible for keeping order is the source of harm? |
| Collective grief vs private guilt | Can a community mourn together while an individual hides their part in the loss? |
| Reputation vs responsibility | Will I admit the truth if it makes me look guilty even when I did not cause the original event? |
| Solidarity vs exclusion | Who gets protected by the group, and who is treated as expendable? |

A strong conflict often has more than one valid concern and no cost-free solution.

## 8. Ritual, sound, movement and community pressure

The hive-switch chapters are useful when examining already-established collective activity.

For any existing ritual or procession, record:

1. Who participates, who watches, who is absent and who refuses?
2. What is repeated: movement, sound, phrase, route, object handling or timing?
3. What do participants do in synchrony?
4. What emotion is visible before, during and after participation?
5. Does the activity temporarily soften social differences or reinforce hierarchy?
6. What shared identity does it express?
7. What happens when someone breaks synchrony or refuses to participate?
8. Is the meaning shared, or do participants have different private interpretations?

Do not assume synchronised movement automatically creates loyalty, obedience or violence. Haidt's point is that collective practices can intensify belonging; their social and moral result depends on context, structure and leadership.

## 9. A practical scene audit

For every major conflict scene, ask:

- What is the immediate trigger?
- Which character reacts first, and how?
- What is the first explanation offered?
- What does the character leave unsaid?
- Which relationship is influencing the response?
- Which group norm is present, even if nobody states it?
- What is at risk: love, trust, status, belonging, authority, freedom or sacredness?
- Does the opposing character have a coherent moral reason?
- What new cost follows from the choice?
- Does the scene change a relationship, or merely deliver information?

A scene should not contain all six foundations. One or two active concerns, plus a conflicting concern in another character, may be enough.

## 10. Failure modes to avoid

1. **Psychology as diagnosis:** Do not label a character "a Loyalty type" or "high Sanctity." The foundations are lenses for interpreting moral intuitions, not character classes.
2. **American political import:** Do not translate Haidt's US liberal / conservative patterns into Tamil rural factions.
3. **Villager caricature:** Do not use tradition, religion, authority or ritual as shorthand for stupidity or cruelty.
4. **One-note villainy:** Avoid making every harmful action conscious and cynical. Self-justification is often sincere.
5. **One-note victimhood:** A person can be genuinely harmed and still hold power over someone else.
6. **Cohesion equals virtue:** A tightly bonded group may be supportive, unjust, exclusionary or all three.
7. **Disgust equals truth:** A taboo reaction explains emotional force, not objective moral correctness.
8. **One group, one opinion:** Show differences of generation, role, class, relationship, experience and personal history when those differences are established.
9. **Over-explanation:** The audience should infer the emotional logic through choices, silences, rituals and consequences, not through characters lecturing about psychological theory.
10. **Canon drift:** No new cause, motive, ritual function, supernatural rule or relationship is established by this note.

## 11. The story-development principle to retain

**Make characters protect something real to them, then make those protections collide.**

The most unsettling human conflict may come from people who are not pretending to care. They care deeply, but their moral circles, loyalties, fears and duties are different. A character's blind spot can be the shadow cast by a value that is otherwise admirable.

This is the transferable value of Haidt's book for KARUPPI. It is a framework for asking better questions about existing story material, not a replacement mythology and not a change to the Master Story Bible.
