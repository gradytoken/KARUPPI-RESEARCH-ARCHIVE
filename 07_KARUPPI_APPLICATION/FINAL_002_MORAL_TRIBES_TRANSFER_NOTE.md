# FINAL-002 — Moral Tribes: Conditional KARUPPI Transfer Note

**Source study:** 02_BOOK_STUDIES/FINAL_002_Moral_Tribes.md  
**Source:** Joshua D. Greene, *Moral Tribes: Emotion, Reason, and the Gap Between Us and Them* (Atlantic Books, 2014; US publication 2013)  
**Status:** Research application only. No story canon changes approved.  
**Project boundary:** KARUPPI remains a 1990 Tamil village mystery. The removed historical Story B mythology is not to be reintroduced.

## 1. The central transfer

Greene's most useful contribution is the distinction between two levels of moral conflict:

- **Me versus Us:** individual self-interest versus the needs of a cooperative group.
- **Us versus Them:** two groups with different moral rules trying to live in the same world.

A village can be internally cooperative and externally divided. Its members may genuinely protect one another while holding rules that outsiders experience as unfair, frightening or oppressive.

This gives us a way to write community conflict without making one side stupid, uniformly cruel or secretly selfish.

## 2. The group moral-economy worksheet

For each established group, record:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Shared resource | What is being protected or distributed: land, water, safety, reputation, food, knowledge or something else? |
| Cooperation rule | What must members do to receive the group's protection? |
| Free-rider definition | What behaviour is treated as taking without contributing? |
| Fairness principle | Equal shares, need, effort, seniority, lineage, risk, reciprocity or another rule? |
| Sacred authority | Which person, place, text, custom or object is treated as authoritative? |
| In-group boundary | Who is considered one of us? |
| Out-group boundary | Who is considered outside the moral circle? |
| Punishment | How does the group respond to a perceived defection or violation? |
| Internal dissent | Who disagrees with the group's rule while remaining part of it? |
| External conflict | What happens when another group follows an incompatible rule? |
| Evidence standard | What does the group accept as proof? |
| Cost distribution | Who benefits from the rule and who bears its costs? |

The worksheet is not a template that every community must fill in the same way. It is a method for examining existing story material.

## 3. Character worksheet: gut response, manual reasoning and group pressure

For each major existing character, ask:

1. What is the immediate emotional response?
2. Which relationship or group identity does the response protect?
3. What explicit reason does the character give?
4. Does the reason explain the action, or rationalise a position already taken?
5. What evidence would challenge the character's claim?
6. Would they accept the same rule if the roles were reversed?
7. What harm is visible to them, and what harm is statistically distant or socially excluded?
8. What would the character consider fair if they were not personally affected?
9. When does the character trust intuition, and when might they shift into deliberate reasoning?
10. What would they lose socially if they changed their mind?

Do not diagnose characters as "fast thinkers" or "slow thinkers". The book's dual-process model is a research lens, not a personality taxonomy.

## 4. Conflict patterns to test

| Pattern | Psychological engine | Question to test |
|---|---|---|
| Two sincere systems | Groups disagree about the fair terms of cooperation | What does each group protect, and what does each fear losing? |
| The same rule, opposite positions | Each side defines fairness from its own role | Would they still call it fair if the advantage were reversed? |
| The sacred exception | A local authority makes a rule non-negotiable | Who accepts the authority, and who does not? |
| The costly punisher | A person sacrifices something to enforce a group norm | Is the punishment protecting cooperation or protecting power? |
| The free-rider accusation | A character is accused of taking without giving | Is the accusation evidence-based, or does it protect an existing hierarchy? |
| The identifiable victim | One visible person receives sympathy while many unseen people are ignored | Who becomes emotionally legible, and who remains statistical? |
| The alarming act | A direct physical action triggers more outrage than an indirect action with similar consequences | Is the reaction about the harm, the method, or the actor's identity? |
| The biased compromise | Each side offers a compromise that still favours its own position | What would a genuinely role-neutral standard look like? |
| The factual stalemate | Groups accept only evidence that supports their own authorities | What source could both sides recognise? |
| The moral conversion | A character shifts from protecting their own group to weighing a wider consequence | What relationship, evidence or cost makes the shift possible? |

These are craft patterns, not plot proposals.

## 5. Greene's six practical rules as a scene-audit lens

Greene closes with six rules. They can be converted into story questions without adopting his utilitarian philosophy as the story's moral code:

1. **Consult, but do not trust, moral instincts.** What does the character feel immediately, and what might that feeling be missing?
2. **Rights are not for making arguments; they are for ending arguments.** Is a character invoking a right as a reason, a boundary, a social shield or a threat?
3. **Focus on facts.** What happened, what is remembered, what is assumed, and what can be checked?
4. **Beware biased fairness.** Would the character apply the same rule if their own family or group were on the other side?
5. **Use common currency.** What are the actual consequences for each person affected, including outsiders and people who are not present?
6. **Give.** Does the community's care extend beyond visible insiders, and what does that cost?

The sixth rule is a moral recommendation by Greene, not a demand that every fictional character be altruistic.

## 6. Moral conflict without a cartoon villain

A compelling intergroup conflict can emerge when:

- both sides have internal rules that help them cooperate;
- each side has a credible account of what counts as fairness;
- each side sees a different threat;
- each side has facts that matter, but interprets them differently;
- each side is tempted to describe the other as immoral rather than merely different;
- the cost of compromise falls unevenly across individuals.

A character can be generous within their own community and indifferent to a stranger. Another can defend outsiders but betray a close relative. Another can sincerely pursue fairness while unconsciously favouring their own side.

Contradiction is not a flaw in character design. It is often where the character becomes human.

## 7. Conditional application to existing KARUPPI material

The archive records village restrictions, the nightly torch procession, an abandoned shrine, Arjun's disappearance, Rajan's investigation, Thangam, Selvi's drawings and other unresolved mysteries. Verify exact status and detail against the latest Master Story Bible before using them.

Greene's framework may prompt questions:

- Which village rules support cooperation among residents?
- Which rules create friction with outsiders or dissenters?
- What does the community consider a fair allocation of safety, information, duty or punishment?
- Does a restriction arise from a shared resource problem, a sacred authority, fear of defection, or something else?
- Does the procession reinforce cooperation among participants, and how are nonparticipants treated?
- Who is included in the village's moral circle?
- Does Arjun's disappearance expose different definitions of responsibility?
- What evidence would be accepted by different groups?
- Can Rajan distinguish a genuine fact dispute from a conflict over moral authority?

These are questions only. The book does not establish the reason for the procession, shrine, darkness, disappearance or any other open mystery.

## 8. Scene audit: from tribal intuition to a negotiated outcome

For a major intergroup scene, test this sequence:

**Shared problem → group-specific intuition → moral explanation → fairness dispute → evidence conflict → possible common ground → cost of agreement**

1. **Shared problem:** What issue affects both groups?
2. **Group-specific intuition:** What does each side feel immediately?
3. **Moral explanation:** What principle does each side say it is defending?
4. **Fairness dispute:** How does each side define an equal, deserved or necessary outcome?
5. **Evidence conflict:** Which facts are accepted, rejected or interpreted differently?
6. **Possible common ground:** Is there a value both sides recognise, even if they disagree about its meaning?
7. **Cost of agreement:** What will each group have to concede, and who will bear the cost?

A scene does not need to end in compromise. It should make the disagreement intelligible and show what resolution would demand.

## 9. What must not be transferred

- Do not treat utilitarianism or "maximum happiness" as the story's official moral law.
- Do not assume all moral disputes are simple Us-versus-Them conflicts.
- Do not portray local beliefs or sacred commitments as inherently irrational.
- Do not assume group loyalty makes people cruel, or that impartiality makes people automatically wise.
- Do not reduce well-being to money, status or comfort.
- Do not present brain regions as simple moral switches.
- Do not diagnose characters through the dual-process model.
- Do not use cross-cultural game results to rank or stereotype Tamil communities.
- Do not force existing KARUPPI characters into a utilitarian conversion arc.
- Do not use Greene's examples as new plot events or supernatural explanations.
- Do not create mythology, secret histories, motives or causal links from this framework.

## 10. Principle to retain

**Build groups whose internal rules make sense to their members, then place those rules in conflict over something both groups need or care about.**

Greene gives us a research method for understanding how cooperation can become division and how deliberate reasoning may expose biased fairness, selective empathy and competing moral systems. The book's proposed solution remains a philosophical position to examine, not a rule that KARUPPI must endorse.
