# ISR-009 — The Inugami Curse

**Author:** Seishi Yokomizo (1902–1981)  
**Japanese title:** *Inugamike no Ichizoku*  
**English translator:** Yumiko Yamazaki  
**Original Japanese book edition:** 1972, Kadokawa Corporation (copyright / first-publication statement in the supplied book's copyright page)  
**English edition:** Pushkin Press / Pushkin Vertigo, 2020  
**Supplied copy:** 246-page PDF, English translation  
**Study status:** Full-arc study completed, including final reconstruction and closing ambiguity

## 1. Source identity and method

The supplied PDF contains the complete English text, contents page, character list, translator's acknowledgments, author note and copyright page. The English translation is credited to Yumiko Yamazaki. The copyright page identifies the original Japanese title as *Inugamike no Ichizoku*, copyright 1972, first published in Japan by Kadokawa Corporation. The Japan Foundation, New Delhi Library catalogue independently lists the English title, translator, original-language title, 1972 copyright date and 2020 Pushkin Press edition.

The narrative is set in 1940s Japan. The year in the opening is partially obscured in the English text as “194_”; the book's setting should therefore be described as the 1940s unless a specific year is established from another edition or authoritative source. The central plot dates run from October to December.

The book was read through to the final chapter. The study tracks the family structure, will, sequence of attempted killings and murders, clues, alibis, identity changes, postwar movement, confession and final scene. It distinguishes:
- what the novel states happened;
- what Kindaichi infers before the solution;
- what the final reconstruction confirms;
- what remains unresolved at the end.

No long copyrighted passages are reproduced here. Page references refer to the supplied 246-page English PDF, not the Japanese edition's pagination.

## 2. The book's actual genre and central design

*The Inugami Curse* is a **classic family-inheritance detective mystery with gothic and folkloric staging**. It uses a curse narrative, shrine heirlooms, a decaying ancestral house, a lake, winter landscape and gruesome symbolic murders. But the murders are committed by human beings with material, emotional and retaliatory motives.

The title may lead a reader to expect a story about a supernatural dog-spirit curse. That is not the mechanism of this novel. Its central “curse” is Kikuno Aonuma's retaliatory declaration after she and her infant son are brutally abused by Sahei Inugami's three daughters. The heirlooms and the curse give the murders their symbolic pattern; people create, exploit and manipulate that pattern.

The central design has three interlocking engines:

1. **Inheritance:** Sahei's will turns Tamayo's choice of husband into the gateway to a vast fortune.
2. **Revenge:** Kikuno's curse gives the three heirlooms a violent meaning that can be staged as a sequence.
3. **Identity:** Shizuma and Kiyo exploit their resemblance, masks, clothing and changing roles, making genuine physical evidence point toward the wrong identity.

Matsuko is the principal murderer. Shizuma and Kiyo repeatedly obscure, relocate or restage evidence for their own motives. The apparent supernatural pattern is therefore produced by human violence, family history and deliberate misdirection.

## 4. Full-arc story map

### A. Sahei's history and the will

The novel opens with Sahei's public life as a successful silk magnate and his private history as an orphaned drifter. As a teenager, he is found at Nasu Shrine by the priest Daini Nonomiya. Daini and his wife Haruyo nurse him, educate him and help him begin his business career. Sahei's gratitude to the Nonomiya household becomes a lifelong emotional obligation.

Sahei never legally marries. His three daughters, Matsuko, Takeko and Umeko, are born to different women, and their husbands enter the Inugami family and manage branches of the business. The family's outward wealth conceals resentment, distrust and a long struggle over authority.

At Sahei's death, the family expects a conventional transfer of power. Instead, the will is to be read only after Kiyo returns from the war. Its terms are extraordinary: Tamayo receives the three heirlooms and the right to choose one of the three grandsons as her husband. The inheritance is tied to that choice. If Tamayo dies or refuses all three candidates, another branch, Kikuno's son Shizuma, may benefit under a separate provision.

The will is not merely a legal document. It is the mechanism that redirects old grievances into immediate action.

### B. The warning, the accidents and Wakabayashi's murder

Kindaichi is summoned to Nasu by Wakabayashi, who fears a series of impending murders. On the day Kindaichi arrives, Tamayo suffers a suspicious boating accident. Monkey tells him this is the third alarming incident involving her:
- a viper was placed in her bedding;
- the brakes on her car were interfered with;
- a hole was bored in her boat and temporarily plugged.

Tamayo calls them accidents. Monkey sees a pattern. Kindaichi begins to connect the danger to Wakabayashi's warning.

Wakabayashi is found dead in the inn's restroom. The autopsy reveals that the poison entered through his lungs: he smoked a poisoned cigarette. The method allows the killer to poison him without being present when he dies. Later, Matsuko admits that she had given Wakabayashi the poisoned cigarette because he knew she had secretly obtained and read the will.

This establishes that the apparently separate accidents and first murder belong to the same attempt to control the inheritance.

### C. The three heirlooms and the curse

The Inugami heirlooms are an axe, a koto (zither) and a chrysanthemum. The novel links their names through a wordplay sequence that is also associated with the idea of hearing good tidings.

The heirlooms had been kept at Nasu Shrine and were later associated with Sahei's family. Kikuno, who was Sahei's former lover, had received them after she gave birth to Shizuma. The three daughters discovered this and attacked Kikuno in the presence of her infant son. They beat and humiliated her, threatened the child, forced a false statement about the child's paternity and took the heirlooms back.

Kikuno's curse identifies the axe, zither and chrysanthemum as the means by which the three daughters will eventually be undone. The novel turns this statement into a murder pattern:
- Také's severed head is placed on a chrysanthemum doll.
- A koto string is placed around Tomo's neck after he has been strangled by another means.
- The final body is staged upside down as a visual riddle that points to the axe.

The curse is not shown as an independent supernatural force. It is a threat that Shizuma deliberately turns into theatrical staging, and that Matsuko later exploits for concealment.

### D. The first murder: Také

Také and Tomo suspect that the masked Kiyo may be an impostor. They retrieve a votive handprint kept at Nasu Shrine and use it as a reference for checking the returned man's identity.

Tamayo also attempts to use a pocket watch associated with Kiyo. The watch has a fingerprint that could help distinguish the genuine Kiyo from the man wearing the mask. She gives it to Také after a tense encounter at the boathouse's observation deck.

Matsuko attacks Také from behind and stabs him. During the attack, the pocket watch falls from Také's pocket. Matsuko takes it without understanding its full evidentiary significance and hides it in the tobacco drawer of her tray.

Shizuma and Kiyo then manipulate the aftermath. Také's body is moved by boat, and his head is severed and placed on a chrysanthemum doll. A bloody boat and a returned-soldier disguise direct attention toward an outsider. The staging suggests a symbolic ritual murder, but the physical event is a human killing followed by deliberate evidence manipulation.

### E. The handprint trap and the second identity

The family eventually obtains a new handprint from the man wearing the mask. Forensics compares it with the shrine's old handprint and reports a match.

Kindaichi accepts the result too readily. The mistake is not that the handprint comparison is false. It is that the comparison is treated as proof that the same person has occupied the Kiyo identity throughout the whole sequence.

Kiyo and Shizuma look exceptionally alike. They have been in Burma at the same time, where Shizuma learns Kiyo's background and the two develop a relationship. Shizuma, whose face has been severely injured, assumes Kiyo's identity. Later the men exchange roles and use the rubber mask and a concealed face to sustain the deception. The novel's double impersonation makes the identity of a person at one moment different from the identity of the person who appeared in the same role earlier.

The handprint proves something real about the person who made it. It does not, by itself, prove the continuity of identity across all the scenes Kindaichi is trying to connect.

### F. The second murder: Tomo

Tomo tries to abduct Tamayo and sexually assault her. Kiyo, hiding in the abandoned Inugami house in Toyohata Village, intervenes, overpowers Tomo and ties him to a chair. Kiyo phones Monkey to rescue Tamayo.

Monkey retrieves Tamayo but leaves Tomo bound and gagged. Tomo eventually frees himself and returns to the Inugami estate by rowboat. There, Matsuko encounters him near the boathouse and strangles him with her kimono sash.

Kiyo and Shizuma witness the killing. They attempt to revive Tomo but fail. To protect Matsuko, they move his corpse back to the abandoned Toyohata house, strip and bind it to resemble the earlier restraint, and add a koto string around his neck.

Kindaichi notices that the rope burns on Tomo's body do not fit the tightness of the rope as the corpse is found. The discrepancy means the body was re-tied after Tomo had struggled free. A missing chrysanthemum-shaped button from Tomo's shirt is also important. It is eventually found with Sayoko and carries a trace of human blood.

Kokin Miyakawa, Matsuko's koto teacher, has noticed that Matsuko injured her forefinger the previous evening, before she claimed to have hurt it when a koto string snapped during the later lesson. The button, the blood and the false account of the finger injury connect Matsuko to Tomo's death.

### G. The Chinese chest and Tamayo's parentage

At Nasu Shrine, Oyama discovers a sealed Chinese chest containing documents from Sahei and Daini. These documents reveal the hidden relationship between Sahei and Haruyo. Noriko, publicly recognised as Daini's daughter, is biologically Sahei's daughter. Tamayo is therefore Sahei's granddaughter, not simply the granddaughter of his benefactor.

This revelation changes the emotional and legal meaning of the will. It also creates a kinship barrier between Tamayo and Shizuma: Shizuma is Sahei's son through Kikuno, while Tamayo is Sahei's granddaughter through Haruyo. They are uncle and niece by blood, even though the family register does not publicly show the relationship.

Shizuma's revenge and inheritance ambition are therefore caught in a dilemma. He wants to take the place of Kiyo and marry Tamayo, but the newly revealed family connection makes that morally unacceptable to him.

### H. The third body and the final identity test

On December 13, a body is discovered frozen upside down in the lake near the boathouse. Its arrangement is a visual word puzzle: the name Kiyo, inverted, yields the sound sequence associated with the axe. The body appears to fulfil the third element of Kikuno's curse, but there is no corresponding axe wound.

Tamayo asks the medical examiner to take a new handprint from the body. This produces the crucial result: the print does not match the two previous prints. The corpse is not Kiyo. The body is Shizuma, who has been killed by Matsuko and staged as Kiyo.

This removes Kindaichi's blind spot. The earlier handprint was genuine, but it was not proof that the same person had occupied the Kiyo identity in every prior scene.

### I. Kiyo's return, Matsuko's confession and the final outcome

Kiyo is found alive in the Yukigamine Mountains. He tries to take responsibility for the killings, even leaving a signed confession and staging an apparent attempt on Tamayo's life. Kindaichi establishes that Kiyo was in Tokyo at the time Shizuma was murdered and that the attack on Tamayo was a charade intended to make his confession credible and protect Matsuko.

Kiyo explains that he had returned from Burma under an assumed name because of a battlefield failure. As a commander, he made a decision that resulted in the destruction of his unit. He was captured after wandering with the only other survivor, and he felt unable to return publicly under the Inugami name.

Kiyo also describes how he met Shizuma in Burma. The two men had become familiar with one another because of their resemblance. Shizuma, after his facial injury, took Kiyo's place. When Kiyo later returned, he concealed his identity rather than exposing Shizuma immediately.

Matsuko's confession establishes her direct responsibility:
- she arranged the attempts on Tamayo's life;
- she poisoned Wakabayashi with a cigarette;
- she stabbed Také;
- she strangled Tomo;
- she killed Shizuma after learning his true identity and realising that he could not marry Tamayo.

Shizuma and Kiyo were responsible for concealing, relocating and restaging evidence after the killings. Their actions make the investigation far more difficult, but they do not replace Matsuko as the principal murderer.

In the final scene, Tamayo accepts Kiyo as her chosen partner and heir under Sahei's will, even though he is to face imprisonment for his involvement in the cover-ups. She promises to wait for him. Matsuko asks that Sayoko's future child receive half the fortune, and Tamayo agrees.

Matsuko then dies after smoking tobacco from a drawer in her tobacco tray. Kindaichi recognises that the tobacco contains the same poison used to kill Wakabayashi. The novel does not identify who placed the poison there. That final death is not fully explained.

## 5. The clue architecture

| Clue | First interpretation | Later significance |
|---|---|---|
| Viper in Tamayo's bedding | Possible accident / isolated threat | One of Matsuko's attempts to kill Tamayo |
| Tamayo's faulty car brakes | Another accident | Part of the same murder plan |
| Hole in the boat | Boating mishap | A deliberately engineered attempt on Tamayo |
| Poisoned cigarette | A strange form of homicide | Matsuko's attempt to silence Wakabayashi |
| Bloody boat / returning-soldier clues | Possible outside intruder | Evidence deliberately used to redirect suspicion |
| Chrysanthemum doll with severed head | Ritualised murder | Shizuma's use of the heirloom curse as staging |
| Votive handprint | Definitive proof of Kiyo's identity | Genuine evidence misapplied because identities had been exchanged |
| Pocket watch fingerprint | A possible way to detect the impostor | Tamayo's test; later hidden by Matsuko after Také's murder |
| Koto string on Tomo | Apparent murder instrument | Placed around the neck after death to invoke the second heirloom |
| Rope abrasions and tight binding | A detail of the corpse's condition | Shows Tomo had freed himself and was later re-tied |
| Broken koto string / Matsuko's finger | Minor injury during a lesson | Kokin's observation puts Matsuko's injury on the previous night |
| Missing chrysanthemum button | Lost clothing detail | Blood evidence that helps connect Matsuko to Tomo |
| Upside-down body | A bizarre supernatural-looking murder | A staged word puzzle implying the axe |
| New handprint from the final corpse | Routine identity check | Exposes that the corpse is Shizuma, not Kiyo |
| Poisoned tobacco in the final scene | A sudden death | Same poison as Wakabayashi; poisoner remains unidentified |

### The most important reasoning lesson

**A clue can be authentic and still support the wrong conclusion.**

The handprint is the clearest example. It is not forged. It is not technically defective. The error lies in assuming that a valid physical match proves the social identity of a person across multiple appearances.

The novel also distinguishes the person who commits a crime from the person who rearranges the scene afterward. Kindaichi is misled because he sees a complex pattern of hidden evidence and assumes the killer must be the one orchestrating every concealment. In fact, the killer is less concerned about hiding her crimes than the two men who are cleaning up after her.

## 6. Mystery and suspense techniques worth studying

### 6.1 A will as an active plot mechanism

The will is not background information. It creates a contest with specific consequences, deadlines and possible beneficiaries. Every new revelation changes the value of a relationship. Tamayo's marriage choice becomes the central pressure point, and the family tree is part of the mystery's mechanism.

### 6.2 One event, several competing explanations

The boat incident can be read as an accident, an attack, or part of a larger plan. The murders can be read as supernatural retaliation, a family member's greed, or an outside person's revenge. The narrative lets these models coexist until physical evidence begins to separate them.

### 6.3 Recurring symbols that change function

The three heirlooms give the reader a pattern to recognise. However, the pattern is not a simple ritual repetition. Each symbol is staged differently:
- an actual object/image in the first murder;
- an attached symbolic trace in the second;
- a visual pun in the third.

The pattern becomes a form of communication, but also a weapon of misdirection.

### 6.4 The investigator's blind spot is logical, not supernatural

Kindaichi does not fail because he lacks information. He fails because he assigns too much certainty to an identity test. His mistake emerges from a reasonable assumption that the novel then overturns.

### 6.5 The landscape participates in the mechanics

The lake, sluice gate, boat shed, reed beds, abandoned house and winter ice are not just atmospheric scenery. They govern:
- how bodies can be moved;
- which routes are visible;
- where a person can hide;
- how long evidence remains concealed;
- when a body resurfaces;
- how a staged corpse can be positioned.

The change from autumn lake to winter ice also changes the visual and physical logic of the investigation.

### 6.6 Public gossip and economic dependency create pressure

The Inugami Group dominates the local economy. The town's prosperity is linked to the family's business, so residents follow the family's succession struggle with intense interest. This makes the family mystery a public event. People are not merely curious about rich strangers: their own livelihoods are entangled with the family's fortunes.

### 6.7 Evidence is introduced as an evolving chain

The narrative repeatedly returns to earlier details and changes their meaning. The watch, button, broken koto string, handprint and tobacco are not disposable clues. Each has a second function that becomes visible after later testimony or a new physical finding.

## 7. Character and social dynamics

### Inheritance as a system of coercion

Sahei's will appears to compensate Tamayo for the hidden history of the Nonomiya family. But it also turns marriage into a legal gateway to wealth. It makes the young woman's choice carry disproportionate financial consequences for several branches of the family.

The will is both a gesture of restitution and a mechanism that provokes further violence. The novel recognises this contradiction through Kindaichi's unease about the tragedies caused by Sahei's final decision.

### The town as an economic organism

The novel repeatedly describes the town's dependence on the Inugami business. When the silk trade weakens, the region and the old Toyohata settlement decline. The abandoned ancestral house becomes a physical record of the family's business history and of the area's economic reversal.

This is a useful example of a mystery where local power extends beyond the family home. The town's attention, gossip and financial dependence make the family conflict socially consequential.

### Women and agency

Tamayo is often described through beauty and silence, and the narration sometimes frames her in a highly objectifying way. Yet she is not simply passive: she creates an independent fingerprint test, withholds what she suspects, and eventually insists on a new identity check.

Matsuko, meanwhile, is an agent of extreme violence and strategy, but her motive is shaped by her attachment to her son and by the inheritance system. The text explores female power through family property and maternal loyalty, but it also narrows women into beauty, jealousy, marriage and motherhood more often than a contemporary story would need to.

Sayoko's pregnancy and breakdown are used as emotional and investigative turning points. Her experience is not treated with much psychological depth beyond its function in the plot.

### Sexuality and period framing

The documents found in the Chinese chest reveal a complicated relationship among Sahei, Daini and Haruyo. The narrator uses moralising and pathologising language to describe same-sex desire and the unconventional relationship. That language belongs to the novel's period framing and should not be adopted as neutral analysis.

For research purposes, the valuable material is the secret lineage, the social pressure surrounding marriage, the concealment of paternity and the power of family records. The novel's moral labels should be read critically.

## 8. Setting and period context

### What the novel itself supplies

The book gives us a specific postwar rural setting:
- a wealthy family tied to the silk industry;
- an economically dependent lakeside town;
- an abandoned earlier family home in Toyohata;
- train stations, a hotel, a boathouse, a sluice gate and local boats;
- a shrine that keeps historical family objects and a votive handprint;
- a war-returnee population and uncertainty surrounding missing soldiers;
- a landscape that shifts from autumn rain to freezing winter and snow.

### Independent historical context

The historical background of mass repatriation is real. Lori Watt's *When Empire Comes Home: Repatriation and Reintegration in Postwar Japan* documents the return of millions of Japanese nationals from across Asia after the war and examines how the returning population was socially and politically understood. The Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's historical account likewise describes postwar confusion, returning soldiers, unemployment, orphans, shortages and difficult living conditions.

This supports the broader credibility of a postwar setting in which identities, service records, family reunions and local reintegration are uncertain. It does **not** independently validate the novel's specific fictional circumstances, its precise characters or their individual war records.

### The word “Inugami” and actual folklore

There is a real Japanese folk-belief tradition called *inugami*. A 1998 article by Masanobu Kagawa in *Seishin Igaku* describes inugami possession traditions particularly associated with Tokushima and Kochi in Shikoku and Oita in Kyushu. It discusses beliefs about animal-like spirits, inherited “inugami” family lines and the social stigma associated with such families.

That tradition is **not the plot mechanism of this novel**. The fictional Inugami family is a wealthy business clan in the novel's Nasu / Shinshu setting. The curse that drives the murders is Kikuno's explicit revenge curse involving three heirlooms. We must not conflate the novel's family name and curse plot with the regional Japanese inugami-possession tradition.

The novel also describes a Nasu Shrine votive handprint associated with men going to war. That is a source-specific custom as presented by the author. This study has not independently verified it as a widespread Shinto practice or as a standard Japanese military custom.

## 10. Strengths, weaknesses and limitations

### Strengths

- The will creates immediate stakes and governs the character relationships.
- The family tree is not merely background: it affects motive, eligibility, identity and the final solution.
- The clues have more than one function and are reinterpreted as the investigation progresses.
- The crime scenes are shaped by landscape, weather, architecture and access.
- The main culprit and the people covering for her have different motives, which complicates the evidence trail.
- The novel makes a real forensic result misleading without making it fraudulent.
- Public gossip and economic dependency give the private family conflict a wider social consequence.
- The final revelation changes how the reader understands earlier testimony and behaviour.

### Weaknesses and risks

- The long opening history and the many family relationships demand careful reader attention before the main investigation takes off.
- Some scenes lean heavily on melodrama, coincidence and elaborate identity switches.
- The solution relies on multiple role exchanges and the reader must mentally reconstruct who occupied which role at each time.
- The novel sometimes uses beauty, sexual jealousy and coercive marriage as the primary way to frame its female characters.
- The description of the Daini–Sahei–Haruyo relationship uses dated moral and sexual language.
- The detective's reasoning sometimes depends on dramatic timing and late testimony rather than a fully transparent chain of independently testable evidence.
- The final poison death of Matsuko is not resolved. The same poison used on Wakabayashi is found in the tobacco she takes from the drawer, but the book does not identify who placed it there.

## 11. What the book teaches us

1. **A curse can be an organising story rather than a supernatural cause.** People may use a curse to explain, stage or conceal violence.
2. **A family tree can be a mystery mechanism.** Hidden paternity and unacknowledged relationships can change the meaning of legal documents and apparent motives.
3. **A clue is not the same as its interpretation.** A genuine handprint does not automatically prove the identity of every person who used the same role.
4. **The person who stages a crime may not be the person who committed it.** Keep the murderer, helper, evidence mover and witness distinct.
5. **A recurring motif needs a changing function.** Repetition works best when each recurrence alters the reader's understanding.
6. **A town can have an economic stake in a family's secrets.** Social curiosity becomes more credible when livelihoods are connected to the family.
7. **Setting should constrain the crime.** A lake, sluice gate, frozen water, abandoned building and train schedule determine what is possible.
8. **An investigator needs a timeline, not only a theory.** Movement, time of death, access and witness limitations gradually undo the false narrative.
9. **The witness who does not speak may possess the critical fact.** Tamayo's hesitation is not empty withholding; it follows from her emotional investment and partial recognition.
10. **Not every final detail must be closed.** The unexplained poison in Matsuko's tobacco tray leaves a final disturbance after the main murder structure has been solved.

## 12. Overall assessment for research

**ISR-009 is a high-value mystery-architecture study and a medium-value community / setting study. It is not a direct folklore reference book.**

Its most useful contribution is the construction of a layered investigation in which:
- inheritance creates the motive field;
- family history creates hidden identities;
- local objects create a symbolic pattern;
- the landscape controls the physical logistics;
- genuine evidence produces a false conclusion;
- and a second layer of helpers hides the first layer of crimes.

Its strongest lesson is not “make the village cursed.” It is: **make the belief system, family structure, physical evidence and human incentives interact so tightly that the audience can initially mistake one for another.**

No KARUPPI story canon changes are made by this study.
