## 1. Why this book belongs in the final research sequence

The two psychology books already studied give us two complementary scales of human behaviour:

- Jonathan Haidt's *The Righteous Mind* explains how moral intuitions, social identities and group loyalties bind people into moral communities, while those same structures can divide communities.
- Viktor Frankl's *Man's Search for Meaning* examines individual purpose, responsibility, love, suffering and the effort to retain direction under severe constraint.

Joshua Greene's *Moral Tribes* adds a bridge between those scales. It asks why people cooperate within their own groups yet struggle to cooperate across groups whose moral commitments differ. It then asks whether human beings can deliberately construct a higher-level way of resolving those disagreements.

For KARUPPI, the book is valuable because it gives us a model of **conflict between sincerely held moral systems**. Two groups may both value fairness, safety, duty, dignity, family and social order, while disagreeing about what those values require. A conflict need not begin with one group being openly selfish or evil. It may begin because each group understands cooperation on different terms.

Greene also adds a useful distinction between the psychological machinery that produces fast moral responses and the more flexible reasoning people can use to compare competing claims. That distinction is helpful for building characters who feel one thing immediately, reason toward another position, and then struggle with the social cost of changing their minds.

The book's proposed solution, however, is not neutral scientific fact. Greene argues for utilitarianism as a "common currency" and calls his mature proposal **deep pragmatism**. That is a philosophical position supported by his reading of psychological evidence. It must be studied as an argument, not treated as a scientifically established moral law.

**Primary story-research value:** Create conflict between groups who each have internally coherent moral systems, then examine what happens when their rules collide over a shared problem.

## 2. Edition structure and the book's five-part argument

The supplied contents page organises the argument into five parts and twelve chapters (PDF pp. 7-8):

| Part | Chapters | Main question | Story-research use |
|---|---:|---|---|
| I. Moral Problems | 1-3 | Why does morality enable cooperation inside groups yet contribute to conflict between groups? | Shared resources, group rules, competing moral worlds, tribal loyalty |
| II. Morality Fast and Slow | 4-5 | How do automatic emotional responses and deliberate reasoning interact in moral judgment? | Impulsive decisions, moral disgust, direct violence, rationalisation, delayed reconsideration |
| III. Common Currency | 6-8 | Can groups find a higher-level standard for resolving moral disagreement? | Compromise, shared outcomes, conflicting values, community trade-offs |
| IV. Moral Convictions | 9-10 | Are our strongest moral intuitions reliable, especially when actions feel violent or unfair? | Taboo, punishment, perceived justice, empathy, identifiable victims |
| V. Moral Solutions | 11-12 | How should people reason when their tribes disagree? | Factual disputes, principled compromise, rights, authority, decisions under group pressure |

Greene uses three recurring metaphors:

1. **The Tragedy of the Commons:** the conflict between Me and Us, where individual self-interest can damage a cooperative group.
2. **The Tragedy of Commonsense Morality / the New Pastures:** the conflict between Us and Them, where moral groups disagree about the proper terms of cooperation.
3. **The dual-mode camera:** automatic settings and manual mode, representing efficient but inflexible responses and slower, more flexible reasoning.

The final idea is **common currency**: a standard Greene believes can help people compare competing moral claims without simply giving victory to whichever group has the strongest inherited intuition.

## 3. Introduction: The Tragedy of Commonsense Morality
**PDF pp. 9-24**

### 3.1 The New Pastures parable

Greene opens with four fictional herding tribes around a forest:

- The Eastern tribe allocates equal numbers of sheep to each family.
- The Western tribe allocates sheep according to family size.
- The Northern tribe divides the land into private plots, allowing different levels of wealth and ownership.
- The Southern tribe shares land and livestock collectively, with elders assigning work and distributing output.

Each system has strengths and recurring complaints. The East faces disputes about equal shares and cheating. The West faces arguments over family size and unequal need. The North faces inequality, inheritance, luck and poverty. The South faces questions about effort, free riding, and the costs of collective management.

A fire destroys the forest. New grassland appears, and all four groups claim the new pastures. Their internal rules, which had helped each tribe cooperate, now conflict with the rules of neighbouring tribes. Disputes over land, property, customs, sacred claims and local authority escalate into violence.

The parable is a constructed thought experiment. It is not an ethnographic description of actual cultures. Its value is that it shows how different systems can be internally stable and still collide when they share a common resource.

### 3.2 Two tragedies

Greene distinguishes:

| Problem | Conflict | What fails | Greene's shorthand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tragedy of the Commons | Me versus Us | Individuals pursue immediate advantage and undermine a shared resource or cooperative enterprise | Cooperation within a group |
| Tragedy of Commonsense Morality | Us versus Them | Groups defend different moral systems and struggle to agree on shared rules | Cooperation between groups |

The central paradox is that morality helps a group overcome selfishness internally, but the same loyalty, rules and shared identity may intensify competition with outsiders.

Greene describes the first problem as nature's problem of cooperation. He argues that nature did not provide an automatic solution to the second. To resolve conflicts between groups, people may need a deliberately constructed higher-level moral standard, which he calls a **metamorality** (PDF pp. 20-32).

### 3.3 The main question

The book is not simply asking how to make people less tribal. It asks how groups with genuinely different values can decide what to do when they share a world and must live with one another's decisions.

**Story implication:** A community can be cohesive, caring and morally serious inside its own boundary, while being unable to recognise the legitimacy of another group's moral world.

## 4. Part I — Moral Problems

# Chapter 1: The Tragedy of the Commons
**PDF pp. 25-32**

### 4.1 The cooperation problem

Greene uses Garrett Hardin's pasture parable. Each herder receives the full private benefit of adding an animal, while the cost of overgrazing is shared. Each individual's decision appears rational, but the combined result destroys the resource.

The problem appears wherever individual and collective interests are partly aligned but not identical. Greene gives examples involving trade, relationships, nonaggression and even cooperation within the human body, where cells that pursue their own interests can produce cancer.

### 4.2 Morality as cooperation machinery

Greene offers this functional definition:

> Morality is a set of psychological adaptations that allow otherwise selfish individuals to reap the benefits of cooperation.

The definition is functional rather than a complete normative account of what is morally right. It asks what moral tendencies do in human social life.

Greene argues that cooperation requires people to limit immediate self-interest and accept some personal cost for others or for the group.

### 4.3 The evolutionary caveat

Greene argues that cooperative tendencies can evolve when they help one group outperform another. He stresses that this does not mean morality evolved to make everyone cooperate universally. The evolutionary story is compatible with cooperation within a group and competition between groups.

He also says that humans can use evolved capacities in ways that go beyond their evolutionary origins. For example, people can help distant strangers without expecting a reciprocal return.

**Research distinction:** An explanation of why a tendency evolved is not the same as a justification for whether we should follow it. Greene uses this distinction repeatedly.

**Story implication:** The same practice can protect a community internally while making it suspicious or hostile toward outsiders. Do not assume that a group's internal kindness automatically generalises to everyone.

# Chapter 2: Moral Machinery
**PDF pp. 33-66**

Greene simplifies cooperation using the Prisoner's Dilemma and his recurring characters Art and Bud.

### 4.4 The "magic corner"

The magic corner is the outcome in which participants cooperate and both receive a better result than if each pursues immediate individual advantage.

Greene describes several pieces of moral machinery that help people reach and remain in that corner.

| Mechanism | How Greene explains it | Potential dramatic expression |
|---|---|---|
| Kin care | Genetic relatedness supports costly care for relatives | Family protection, inheritance, sacrifice |
| Reciprocal altruism | Repeated cooperation makes future benefit more valuable than one-time betrayal | Trust built over time, revenge after betrayal |
| Tit for Tat | Begin cooperatively, then respond to the other person's previous action | Conditional friendship, testing loyalty |
| Vengeful anger | A credible willingness to retaliate can deter exploitation | Threats, reputation, deterrence |
| Guilt and shame | Internal discomfort can make promises credible even when no one is watching | Secret guilt, self-punishment, fear of exposure |
| Friendship and love | Emotional commitment can keep people loyal when a better short-term option appears | Staying with a person despite cost, divided loyalties |
| Third-party enforcement | An authority can reward cooperation and punish defection | Elders, leaders, councils, institutions |
| Pro-social punishment | People may pay a personal cost to punish those who exploit the group | Public sanctions, moral outrage, punishment of rule-breakers |
| Tribal identity | Group markers help people know who belongs to the cooperative network | Clothing, symbols, accent, family name, ritual membership |

### 4.5 Emotions as commitment devices

An important insight is that emotions are not merely irrational disturbances. In Greene's account, anger, guilt, shame, gratitude, love and loyalty can function as commitment devices. They make certain choices emotionally difficult, which can help stabilise long-term cooperation.

A person who would otherwise betray a partner for a better deal may remain loyal because love or honour makes betrayal unacceptable to them.

This does not mean that every emotional commitment is good. A device that stabilises cooperation within one relationship or group can also stabilise harmful obligations.

### 4.6 Group membership and arbitrary boundaries

Greene describes experiments in which people favour randomly assigned groups, even when the groups have no meaningful history. He discusses social categorisation, team markers, minimal-group research and the way oxytocin may increase cooperation toward insiders without automatically increasing concern for outsiders.

His conclusion is deliberately qualified: people are disposed toward tribal categorisation, but they are not permanently locked into one fixed group boundary. Experience, learning and context can alter which group markers matter.

### 4.7 Punishment and the public goods game

Greene describes public-goods experiments in which people contribute to a common pool. Free riders can benefit from others' contributions while withholding their own. When costly punishment is introduced, contribution levels often rise.

The willingness to punish a free rider can support group cooperation, even when the punisher does not receive a direct material reward.

**Story implication:** A character may punish a rule-breaker because they feel that the group itself has been injured. The punishment may be sincere and costly, not simply an attempt to gain power. The question is whether the rule being defended is itself fair.

# Chapter 3: Strife on the New Pastures
**PDF pp. 67-100**

### 4.8 Two sources of intergroup conflict

Greene identifies two broad sources:

1. **Tribalism:** favouring insiders over outsiders.
2. **Disagreement about the terms of cooperation:** groups genuinely disagree about what people owe one another and what a fair society should look like.

These can blend. A group may prefer a moral rule partly because it benefits the group, while sincerely believing that the rule is universally fair.

### 4.9 Biased fairness

Greene uses the term **biased fairness** for a subtle process: people want to be fair, but unconsciously favour the version of fairness that best suits their own position.

His examples include legal-cost questions phrased from opposing roles, negotiation experiments in which plaintiffs and defendants estimate the same case differently, and labour negotiations where unions and school boards choose different comparison districts.

The result is not always deliberate lying. People can sincerely perceive the facts differently because they occupy different positions.

### 4.10 Different moral economies

Greene describes cross-cultural economic games, including the Dictator Game, Public Goods Game and Ultimatum Game. The experiments reported in the book show considerable variation across small-scale societies and industrialised populations.

He argues that these differences are related to how people live: the benefits of cooperation in a particular economy, the degree of market integration, and the form of social life.

The lesson is not that one culture is naturally generous and another selfish. The game responses reflect social and economic contexts.

### 4.11 Local moral values and "proper nouns"

Some moral disagreements concern values tied to particular authorities, sacred texts, people, places or traditions. Greene calls these local moral commitments. They may seem arbitrary to outsiders but be central to the moral world of insiders.

He uses the Danish cartoon controversy and religious restrictions as examples of conflict over sacred symbols and authority. He argues that religious traditions can provide shared moral commitments, such as versions of the Golden Rule, while also grounding local rules that divide groups.

### 4.12 Moral perceptions are biased too

People may select or remember facts that fit their group's story. Greene describes how group affiliation can shape what people accept as evidence, and how an argument about facts can become an argument about loyalty.

**Story implication:** Two witnesses may disagree not because one is lying, but because each is filtering an event through a different cooperative world. The audience should be able to distinguish the observable event, the witness's perception, the group's interpretation and the interest served by the interpretation.

## 5. Part II — Morality Fast and Slow

# Chapter 4: Trolleyology
**PDF pp. 101-123**

### 5.1 The switch and footbridge dilemmas

Greene studies familiar trolley problems:

- In the **switch case**, diverting a trolley kills one person but saves five.
- In the **footbridge case**, pushing a person into the trolley's path would stop it and save five.

Many people approve of the switch action and reject the footbridge action, despite the apparent similarity in the number of lives saved.

### 5.2 The dual-process account

Greene proposes that moral judgment involves two interacting processes:

| Mode | Greene's camera metaphor | Typical function |
|---|---|---|
| Automatic settings | Fast, emotional and efficient | Respond to familiar cues using learned or evolved responses |
| Manual mode | Slower, controlled and flexible | Apply explicit decision rules to novel or complex problems |

In the trolley examples, Greene argues that the footbridge case activates stronger emotional responses than the switch case. The controlled reasoning system can support a utilitarian calculation, but the emotional response can oppose it.

He connects this account to research on brain regions involved in emotion and cognitive control, including the VMPFC, amygdala and DLPFC. He describes fMRI work, studies of patients with brain damage, cognitive-load experiments, time-pressure experiments and other manipulations.

### 5.3 The evidence and its limits

The evidence described in the book is not all of one kind. Brain imaging is correlational; neurological cases and experimental manipulations provide different kinds of evidence. Greene recounts how follow-up work tried to address the limitations of early imaging studies.

A careful reading should not reduce the chapter to the slogan "emotion is bad, reason is good." Greene's own model requires emotion and controlled cognition. The automatic system is efficient in familiar settings; controlled reasoning is useful for new situations and competing rules.

**Story implication:** A character can react immediately with fear, disgust or loyalty, then reason toward a different choice. The most interesting moment may be the tension between these two systems, especially when the character cannot fully explain why their first response was so strong.

# Chapter 5: Efficiency, Flexibility, and the Dual-Process Brain
**PDF pp. 124-136**

Greene broadens the camera metaphor beyond trolley problems.

### 5.4 Why automatic settings and manual mode coexist

Automatic settings are efficient because they use lessons acquired through:

- biological inheritance;
- cultural learning;
- personal experience.

But automatic settings are limited. They work best when a situation resembles the situations in which the response was learned.

Manual mode provides flexibility for unfamiliar situations. It allows people to consider explicit rules and weigh competing possibilities.

### 5.5 Emotion and reasoning

Greene describes emotion as a family of automatic processes that exert pressure on behaviour. Emotions can prepare people to flee, fight, approach, avoid, protect or punish.

Reasoning, in his account, involves the conscious application of decision rules. It is not a fully independent source of motivation. Reasoning operates in a brain already shaped by emotion and goals.

The chapter returns to the idea that "knowing" and "feeling" are not interchangeable. A person may understand a decision intellectually while lacking the emotional signals that usually guide action.

### 5.6 The metacognitive problem

A dual-mode mind needs to know when to rely on automatic settings and when to shift into manual mode. That choice is not itself automatic in every case.

Greene argues that understanding the mind can help people choose when to pause, reconsider and reason through a problem.

**Story implication:** A person can be skilled in ordinary life yet fail when the social situation changes. A habit that worked for years may become a liability when the character faces a new kind of moral conflict.

## 6. Part III — Common Currency

# Chapter 6: A Splendid Idea
**PDF pp. 137-161**

### 6.1 Why relativism is insufficient

Greene argues that saying "different tribes have different values" does not solve the problem of deciding how they should live together. Laws and collective decisions still have to be made.

He proposes a higher-level standard: evaluate social systems by their consequences for the people affected.

### 6.2 Consequentialism and utilitarianism

Greene distinguishes consequentialism from utilitarianism:

- **Consequentialism:** the consequences of actions or policies are ultimately what matter.
- **Utilitarianism:** a particular form of consequentialism that treats overall well-being or happiness as the value to be maximised.

He uses Bentham and Mill as the major historical reference points. He is particularly interested in the impartiality of utilitarian reasoning: everyone's experience counts, not only that of the group to which one belongs.

### 6.3 Happiness as a common currency

Greene argues that people may disagree about rights, fairness, authority or sacred values, yet still recognise the difference between a better and worse experience.

His proposed common currency is the quality of experience: happiness, suffering, well-being and the consequences of decisions for everyone affected.

This is not simply money or material wealth. Greene discusses friendship, learning, relationships, beauty and other experiences that affect how life goes for a person.

### 6.4 The "happiness" problem

The chapter considers whether all forms of happiness are alike and how people compare different kinds of experience. Greene discusses Mill's higher pleasures and suggests that some activities may be valuable because of the long-term resources they build for individuals and others.

**Important qualification:** "Happiness" and "well-being" are contested philosophical concepts. Greene's use of a shared currency is a proposed framework, not a universally agreed measurement system.

# Chapter 7: In Search of Common Currency
**PDF pp. 162-174**

Greene examines competing foundations for moral truth, including appeals to divine authority, pure reason, and supposedly self-evident rights.

His concern is not simply that people disagree. It is that each side may use the very authority disputed by the other side as the reason that the dispute is already settled.

He calls this a problem of access to moral truth. Greene argues that no non-question-begging method has yet provided a reliable way to settle all such disagreements.

He therefore proposes looking for values people can share across groups: experience, happiness and suffering, along with impartial consideration of those affected.

He does not claim that science has proved utilitarianism to be moral truth. The argument is that empirical knowledge and a common account of human experience can improve moral deliberation and challenge some forms of parochial rationalisation.

### 6.5 Why shared language can mislead

Words such as family, freedom, equality, justice, life and human rights may sound like shared values, yet groups can mean very different things by them. Agreement at the level of vocabulary does not guarantee agreement about the rule or decision.

**Story implication:** Two families may both claim to protect family honour, but one may mean protecting a person's life while the other means protecting the family's public name. Shared words can hide incompatible obligations.

# Chapter 8: Common Currency Found
**PDF pp. 175-192**

Greene introduces the "button" thought experiments, beginning with a choice in which pressing a button gives the decision-maker money while an unknown person dies.

He then moves through a sequence of hypothetical choices:

1. Avoid personal suffering.
2. Accept a small cost to avoid greater personal suffering.
3. Prevent serious suffering for another person.
4. Prevent suffering for more people when other factors are equal.
5. Choose the option that creates greater total well-being, even if it helps fewer people.

Greene argues that people across different groups can understand the appeal of increasing well-being when all else is equal. He sees this as evidence of common moral ground.

But the phrase **"if all else is equal"** is crucial. Once a decision activates competing values, intuitions about rights, harm, personal force, family, fairness or loyalty, people may no longer agree on the outcome.

The chapter then argues that utilitarian reasoning is particularly compatible with manual-mode cognition: it offers a flexible standard for comparing outcomes. Greene's view is that automatic moral settings are less uniform and more shaped by group-specific learning.

**Research caution:** The fact that people can understand or agree with a hypothetical principle when all else is equal does not prove that they accept it as an overriding moral rule in every real-world situation.

## 7. Part IV — Moral Convictions

# Chapter 9: Alarming Acts
**PDF pp. 193-233**

### 7.1 Accommodation and reform

Greene uses two strategies to answer objections to utilitarianism:

- **Accommodation:** show that apparent utilitarian absurdities often do not follow in real-world conditions.
- **Reform:** use psychology and cognitive science to question whether some moral intuitions are reliable guides.

### 7.2 Personal force and the means/side-effect distinction

Greene revisits trolley dilemmas, comparing cases in which harm is caused by direct bodily force with cases in which it is caused through a switch or indirect mechanism.

He reports experiments with remote-footbridge, footbridge-switch and pole variations. His interpretation is that direct personal force is a particularly strong trigger for moral alarm.

He also discusses the distinction between causing harm as a means to an end and allowing harm as a foreseen side effect. This tradition is associated with the Doctrine of Double Effect, which Greene examines as a way of organising intuitions rather than assuming that the intuition itself has already been justified.

### 7.3 The modular myopia hypothesis

Greene hypothesises that people have an automatic "action-plan inspector" that responds to cues associated with personally causing violence. It may be useful for preventing casual violence between group members, but it is not necessarily a reliable system for comparing all possible consequences.

The term **myopia** is important: an automatic alarm can respond strongly to one visible feature while missing another relevant feature of the situation.

Greene links this to experiments in which participants simulate violent actions. The physical response can occur even when participants know the simulated action is harmless.

### 7.4 The theoretical leap

The evidence that people have automatic emotional alarms does not, by itself, prove that those alarms are morally irrelevant or that outcome maximisation should always override them. Greene argues that some alarms are heuristics that can be useful in ordinary settings but misleading in unusual cases.

This is an important place to distinguish the psychological result from the philosophical conclusion.

**Story implication:** A character may react more strongly to an act that looks physically direct than to an act that creates the same harm indirectly. The group may also judge a visible act more harshly than a concealed decision with worse consequences.

# Chapter 10: Justice and Fairness
**PDF pp. 234-261**

### 7.5 Is utilitarianism too demanding?

Greene considers Peter Singer's drowning-child example: a person would clearly be wrong to let a nearby child drown to protect an expensive suit, so why is it morally optional to spend similar money on distant people who could be helped?

The chapter discusses the tension between a demanding ideal and the limitations of real people. Greene argues that a practical moral standard cannot require impossible levels of sacrifice, but it should challenge avoidable indifference.

### 7.6 Identifiable victims and statistical victims

Greene describes research on the identifiable-victim effect. People often give more to a specific identified victim than to a statistical group of people in need, even when the number of people helped is greater in the group scenario.

He discusses laboratory and field studies involving determined versus undetermined recipients and a named child whose story attracts sympathy. Adding broad statistics can sometimes weaken the emotional response to the named victim.

The implication is that empathy is powerful but uneven. People respond more strongly to a person they can picture than to many people represented as numbers.

### 7.7 Personal commitments

Greene recognises that people are not neutral resource-allocation machines. They have family relationships, friendships, professional commitments and projects. The chapter considers how special obligations can be understood in relation to the well-being of the people involved and the wider consequences of supporting stable relationships.

### 7.8 Justice, punishment and retribution

Greene explores how punishment can support cooperation, but also become an end in itself. Intuitive punishment is frequently motivated by anger and disgust, not by careful calculations about deterrence or social outcomes.

He argues that people can overvalue retribution because it feels satisfying. A policy that feels just may still produce worse outcomes than a policy that is less emotionally satisfying but more effective.

### 7.9 The wealthitarian fallacy

Greene distinguishes utility or well-being from wealth. A dollar does not have the same effect on every person. An additional sum can make a major difference to someone poor while having little effect on someone already wealthy.

He describes experiments with Jonathan Baron on how people evaluate income distributions versus utility distributions. The book reports that people can understand diminishing marginal value of money in one task, then treat utility as if it were money in another task.

Greene calls this mistake the **wealthitarian fallacy**.

### 7.10 Limits of the chapter's defence

Greene argues that oppression and slavery do not maximise well-being in the real world because the severe losses imposed on victims far outweigh the gains to oppressors. He also acknowledges theoretical objections to utilitarianism "in principle."

The real-world argument is not identical to a proof that every rights-based or deontological concern is mistaken.

**Story implication:** A punishment can look deserved to the community while producing more damage than repair. A rule can distribute resources fairly by one measure but unfairly by another. A group's strong empathy for an identifiable victim may coexist with indifference to people who remain anonymous.

## 8. Part V — Moral Solutions

# Chapter 11: Deep Pragmatism
**PDF pp. 263-314**

### 8.1 Deep pragmatism

Greene gathers his biological, psychological and philosophical arguments under the term **deep pragmatism**.

It is not short-term expediency or a willingness to compromise on every principle. It is a proposed commitment to use a common standard of well-being and impartiality when groups disagree about how to live together.

Greene uses the image of two moral compasses:

- immediate moral feelings and local group commitments;
- a deliberate, impartial standard for comparing the effects of different decisions.

### 8.2 Me versus Us and Us versus Them

Greene argues that the right style of thinking depends on the kind of problem:

| Problem | Greene's advice | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Me versus Us | Trust many everyday moral instincts | Emotions such as guilt, loyalty, gratitude and empathy help restrain selfishness and maintain cooperation |
| Us versus Them | Shift into manual mode | Groups' automatic moral intuitions can point in different directions, so a shared standard and evidence are needed |

Greene suggests controversy is a warning sign that people may be facing an Us-versus-Them problem. This is a heuristic, not a reliable diagnostic rule for every dispute. Some controversies are about facts, some involve power, and some have mixed or asymmetric stakes.

### 8.3 Rights as shields and weapons

Greene argues that rights language is often unhelpful as a way to prove a contested moral conclusion, because the parties may disagree about which rights exist and how they rank.

But he also argues that rights can be useful as firm social commitments. They can protect moral progress, signal boundaries, and mobilise people when debate is no longer productive.

In his account, rights language can sometimes end an argument rather than resolve it.

### 8.4 Facts and evidence

Greene insists that a policy cannot be evaluated without understanding how it works and what it produces. He urges people to ask for evidence and to admit when they do not know enough.

He treats science as an important common currency of fact, while acknowledging that people can reject evidence that challenges their group commitments.

### 8.5 The abortion case study

Greene uses abortion as an extended example of how both sides can rely on strong intuitions, uncertain assumptions and inconsistent arguments. He examines appeals to rights, bodily autonomy, potential life, viability, fetal appearance, movement, souls and the moral significance of animal life.

The point of this section is methodological: expose the intuitions and assumptions beneath a public disagreement, then ask what consequences different policies are likely to produce. It should not be treated as a current legal guide or as a complete account of abortion ethics.

### 8.6 Greene's explicit contrast with Haidt

Greene acknowledges Haidt's influence and agrees with much of the descriptive psychology in *The Righteous Mind*: moral intuitions are shaped by evolution and culture, groups differ in moral emphasis, and people often use reasoning to rationalise their intuitions.

His disagreement is about the role of conscious reasoning. Greene believes manual-mode reasoning has played a more important role in moral progress than Haidt's social intuitionist emphasis may suggest. He argues that reasoning can slowly change moral cultures, even when it rarely changes a person's mind instantly.

Greene also disagrees with the idea that a society automatically benefits from an equal balance of liberal and conservative moral outlooks. He argues for evaluating institutions and policies by their consequences, not by assuming that competing tribes should always receive equal political weight.

This is Greene's own philosophical and political position, not a settled fact about all societies.

### 8.7 His own political position

Greene describes himself as a liberal in the American sense, but says he is a deep pragmatist first. He claims he could change his political position if the evidence supported a different policy.

He distinguishes traditional tribes bound by history and local authorities from broad coalitions bound by more abstract ideals, including liberal and libertarian meta-tribes.

The author is transparent that he has a political outlook. A responsible reading should not silently treat his preferred political direction as the neutral conclusion of science.

**Story implication:** A person may belong to a group because of inherited identity, or because they have adopted an abstract principle that reaches beyond local identity. Those two kinds of belonging can collide within one person as well as between groups.

# Chapter 12: Beyond Point-and-Shoot Morality — Six Rules for Modern Herders
**PDF pp. 315-321**

Greene closes with six practical rules:

| Rule | Greene's wording / central idea | Story-research interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | In moral controversy, consult but do not trust your moral instincts | A strong feeling is evidence about a character's response, not automatic proof that the response is right |
| 2 | Rights are not for making arguments; they are for ending arguments | Some boundaries are expressed as non-negotiable commitments after deliberation or social settlement |
| 3 | Focus on the facts, and make others do the same | Distinguish evidence, memory, assumption and rumour |
| 4 | Beware of biased fairness | People can sincerely adopt the version of fairness that favours their own position |
| 5 | Use common currency | Translate competing claims into their effects on people and their well-being, while being explicit about who counts |
| 6 | Give | Test whether a community's care extends beyond visible insiders and whether people make meaningful sacrifices for distant strangers |

The chapter ends with Greene's hope that a global moral community can look after its members without doing so primarily to gain advantage over other groups.

This is an aspirational philosophical conclusion, not evidence that group rivalry has disappeared or that a single metric can resolve every moral problem.

| Manual mode | Deliberate, flexible reasoning using explicit rules | A character pauses, compares claims and challenges their first response |
| Personal force | Direct bodily action that triggers stronger moral alarm in Greene's trolley research | Visible direct harm may be judged differently from indirect harm |
| Means / side-effect distinction | Whether harm is used as a means or foreseen as a side effect | A character argues that a harmful outcome was not their intended means |
| Identifiable victim effect | A specific person evokes more empathy than statistical victims | One named victim attracts attention while many unseen victims are ignored |
| Common currency | Shared evaluative standard based on experience and well-being | Compare what competing rules do to actual people |
| Metamorality | A higher-level framework for resolving conflict between moral systems | A negotiation rule that does not simply let one group impose its moral code |
| Deep pragmatism | Greene's name for principled, evidence-sensitive utilitarian reasoning | Compromise grounded in consequences rather than social convenience |
| Wealthitarian fallacy | Confusing well-being with money or material resources | Wealth, status or property do not equal the actual quality of people's lives |

## 10. Strengths of the book for story research

- It shows why groups may become internally cooperative and externally hostile at the same time.
- It provides examples of how arbitrary group boundaries can acquire moral importance.
- It demonstrates that conflict can emerge from sincere disagreement, not just greed or deception.
- It explains how anger, guilt, loyalty and punishment can stabilise relationships and communities.
- It distinguishes fast emotional reactions from controlled decision-making without requiring us to make emotion the villain.
- It gives a vocabulary for studying direct harm, indirect harm, means, side effects and moral alarm.
- It shows how fairness judgments can be unconsciously tilted by one's role or position.
- It distinguishes a specific visible victim from a large group of statistical victims.
- It demonstrates how group members can interpret shared words such as justice, equality, family or rights in incompatible ways.
- It gives a practical framework for asking how a policy, rule or community arrangement affects different people.

## 11. Scientific and philosophical limitations

This section is essential. Greene's book contains empirical research, theoretical interpretation and normative philosophy. They should not be blended.

### 11.1 The descriptive-to-normative gap

Experiments showing how people make moral judgments do not by themselves prove which moral judgments are correct. Neural activity associated with a response cannot establish that the response is morally wrong.

Greene does not claim that neuroscience alone proves utilitarianism. His wider argument is that science can challenge some intuitive objections and help people deliberate more clearly. The normative case for utilitarianism still requires philosophical premises about the value of experience, impartiality and consequences.

### 11.2 Dual-process theory is a model, not a simple brain switch

The camera metaphor is useful, but human cognition is more complicated than two separate boxes called emotion and reason. Automatic and controlled processes interact. Reasoning can be motivated by emotion, and emotional responses can be learned and revised.

Greene's own later work revisits and refines the dual-process account of moral judgment. Current scholarship also debates whether trolley-style dilemmas provide strong evidence for distinct fast and slow processes. For the archive, use the model as a framework for asking questions, not as a settled neural diagram.

### 11.3 The common currency is a philosophical proposal

Well-being is itself contested. Philosophers distinguish hedonist, desire-based and objective-list theories, among others. The question of what makes a life go well is not settled by agreeing that people prefer not to suffer.

Greene's common currency is a powerful proposal, but it does not remove every disagreement about how to value meaning, dignity, autonomy, relationships, rights or sacred commitments.

### 11.4 Aggregation and distribution remain difficult

Maximising an overall total can raise difficult questions about how benefits and burdens are distributed. Greene argues that real-world utilitarianism does not support slavery or oppression, and he distinguishes well-being from wealth. However, he acknowledges theoretical or "in principle" objections and does not demonstrate that every rights-based concern has been fully resolved.

### 11.5 Cultural generalisation

The book reports cross-cultural experiments and argues that cooperation norms vary. The game tasks are stylised, and the societies studied are not interchangeable. A game result cannot be turned into a permanent personality trait or a broad moral ranking of a culture.

### 11.6 Laboratory scenarios are not entire social worlds

Trolley dilemmas isolate features such as personal force, distance, intention and outcome. Their controlled structure helps test hypotheses, but real decisions contain relationships, history, power, uncertainty and institutional constraints that a short vignette cannot reproduce.

A later meta-analysis of the means/side-effect distinction reports a small overall effect and identifies moderators such as personal contact. This is a reason to avoid overstating one simple universal rule.

### 11.7 The "Us versus Them" heuristic can be too neat

Greene recommends using intuition for Me-versus-Us problems and manual reasoning for Us-versus-Them controversies. This is an illuminating rule of thumb, but real conflicts may contain both structures at once. An individual may face a moral duty to a family member while also participating in intergroup conflict. Do not classify every controversial issue as an uncomplicated group-versus-group problem.

### 11.8 Rights are not merely rhetorical devices

Greene's claim that rights language often functions as a shield or weapon is provocative. In law and moral philosophy, rights can also be treated as substantive constraints, legal protections and principles that structure public justification. His interpretation is one philosophical position, not a settled account of what rights are.

### 11.9 The author has a normative and political point of view

Greene openly identifies as a liberal deep pragmatist. He argues that his political commitments are revisable by evidence. The reader should still distinguish his conclusions from neutral scientific findings, and should test his examples and assumptions rather than merely adopt his preferred policy direction.

## 12. Comparison with Haidt and Frankl

| Dimension | Haidt — *The Righteous Mind* | Frankl — *Man's Search for Meaning* | Greene — *Moral Tribes* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main scale | Individual moral intuition and group moral identity | Individual existential meaning | Group-to-group moral conflict and higher-level decision |
| Central problem | Why good people disagree and become self-righteous | What gives life direction under suffering and constraint | How groups with different moral systems can live together |
| Key mechanism | Intuition, rationalisation, moral foundations, group binding | Meaning anchors, responsibility, love, attitude toward the unavoidable | Automatic/controlled processes, tribalism, common currency |
| Core question | What is each group protecting? | What is this person living for? | How can competing groups decide together? |
| Story strength | Believable community moral matrices | Deep individual motivation and emotional stakes | Collective conflict, fairness, legitimacy, compromise and consequences |
| Caution | Moral foundations are a theoretical model, not fixed types | Do not glorify suffering or blame victims | Utilitarianism is an argued solution, not a scientific law |

### A particularly useful difference

Haidt tends to emphasise how reasoning can serve intuition and social identity. Greene agrees that this often happens, but gives deliberate reasoning a larger role in moral progress. He believes that, over time, reasoning can help societies question tribal rules and move toward more impartial standards.

For KARUPPI, both views are useful. One tells us why a character defends a belief. The other helps us ask whether that character can ever revise it.

## 13. Conditional KARUPPI application: questions, not canon

The current KARUPPI foundation remains a 1990 Tamil village mystery. The latest Master Story Bible controls the status of every story element. This study does not create new mythology, character motives, causal explanations, secrets, rituals or supernatural rules.

### 13.1 The two-level conflict map

Use Greene's two problems to analyse existing story material:

| Level | Question | Possible dramatic pressure |
|---|---|---|
| Me versus Us | What does a character owe the family or group, and what happens if they act only for themselves? | Betrayal, free riding, neglected duty, punishment |
| Us versus Them | What different moral systems are colliding, and why can neither group accept the other's rule? | Competing traditions, territory, resource allocation, local authority, sacred boundaries |

Do not assume that one group is entirely selfish or another entirely altruistic. Each may have a mixture of collective concern, self-interest, fear and genuinely different ideas about fairness.

### 13.2 A group moral-economy worksheet

For each established group or community within the story, record:

| Field | Research question |
|---|---|
| Shared resource | What is being protected or distributed: land, water, safety, reputation, food, knowledge or something else? |
| Cooperation rule | What must members do to receive the group's protection? |
| Free-rider definition | What behaviour is treated as taking without contributing? |
| Fairness principle | Equal shares, need, effort, seniority, lineage, risk, reciprocity or another rule? |
| Sacred authority | Which person, place, text, custom or object is treated as authoritative? |
| In-group boundary | Who is considered one of us? |
| Out-group boundary | Who is considered outside the moral circle? |
| Punishment | How does the group respond to a perceived defection or violation? |
| Internal dissent | Who disagrees with the group's rule while remaining part of it? |
| External conflict | What happens when another group follows an incompatible rule? |
| Evidence standard | What does the group accept as proof? |
| Cost distribution | Who benefits from the rule and who bears its costs? |

### 13.3 Character worksheet: gut response, manual reasoning and group pressure

For each major existing character:

1. What is the immediate emotional response?
2. Which relationship or group identity does the response protect?
3. What explicit reason does the character give?
4. Does the reason explain the action, or rationalise a position already taken?
5. What evidence would challenge the character's claim?
6. Would they accept the same rule if the roles were reversed?
7. What harm is visible to them, and what harm is statistically distant or socially excluded?
8. What would the character consider fair if they were not personally affected?
9. When does the character trust intuition, and when might they shift into deliberate reasoning?
10. What would they lose socially if they changed their mind?

Do not assume every character has a single stable moral mode. Their responses may change with the threat, the relationship and the audience.

### 13.4 Conflict patterns to test

| Pattern | Psychological engine | Question to test |
|---|---|---|
| Two sincere systems | Groups disagree about the fair terms of cooperation | What does each group protect, and what does each fear losing? |
| The same rule, opposite positions | Each side defines fairness from its own role | Would they still call it fair if the advantage were reversed? |
| The sacred exception | A local authority makes a rule non-negotiable | Who accepts the authority, and who does not? |
| The costly punisher | A person sacrifices something to enforce a group norm | Is the punishment protecting cooperation or protecting power? |
| The free rider accusation | A character is accused of taking without giving | Is the accusation evidence-based, or does it protect an existing hierarchy? |
| The identifiable victim | One visible person receives sympathy while many unseen people are ignored | Who becomes emotionally legible, and who remains statistical? |
| The alarming act | A direct physical action triggers more outrage than an indirect action with similar consequences | Is the reaction about the harm, the method, or the actor's identity? |
| The biased compromise | Each side offers a compromise that still favours its own position | What would a genuinely role-neutral standard look like? |
| The factual stalemate | Groups accept only evidence that supports their own authorities | What source could both sides recognise? |
| The moral conversion | A character shifts from protecting their own group to weighing a wider consequence | What relationship, evidence or cost makes the shift possible? |

These are research prompts, not plot proposals.

### 13.5 Questions to apply to existing KARUPPI elements only

The archive records village restrictions, the nightly torch procession, an abandoned shrine, Arjun's disappearance, Rajan's investigation, Thangam, Selvi's drawings and other unresolved mysteries. Verify the exact details against the latest Master Story Bible before using them.

Greene's framework allows us to ask:

- Which rules help the village cooperate internally?
- Which rules create friction with outsiders or dissenters?
- What does the community consider a fair allocation of safety, information, duty or punishment?
- Is a restriction justified by a shared resource problem, a sacred authority, a fear of defection, or something else?
- Does the procession reinforce cooperation among participants, and how are nonparticipants treated?
- Which members are considered part of the moral circle?
- Does the disappearance of one person expose different definitions of responsibility?
- What evidence would be accepted by different groups?
- Can Rajan distinguish a genuine fact dispute from a conflict over moral authority?

The book does not establish the reason for the procession, the shrine, the darkness, the disappearance or any other open mystery.

## 14. Practical scene audit: from tribal intuition to a negotiated outcome

For a major intergroup scene, test this sequence:

**Shared problem → group-specific intuition → moral explanation → fairness dispute → evidence conflict → possible common ground → cost of agreement**

1. **Shared problem:** What issue affects both groups?
2. **Group-specific intuition:** What does each side feel immediately?
3. **Moral explanation:** What principle does each side say it is defending?
4. **Fairness dispute:** How does each side define an equal, deserved or necessary outcome?
5. **Evidence conflict:** Which facts are accepted, rejected or interpreted differently?
6. **Possible common ground:** Is there a value both sides recognise, even if they disagree about its meaning?
7. **Cost of agreement:** What will each group have to concede, and who will bear the cost?

A scene does not need to end in compromise. The value of the framework is that it makes the disagreement intelligible and gives the audience a way to understand why a solution is difficult.

## 15. Study verdict

**Research value for KARUPPI: Very high.** *Moral Tribes* is the final bridge in our planned book sequence. Haidt helps us understand how moral worlds are formed and defended. Frankl helps us understand what individuals are living for. Greene helps us examine what happens when different moral worlds must share the same social space and cannot agree on the rules.

Its strongest transferable insight is that **cooperation inside a group does not guarantee moral agreement between groups**. A close-knit community can be generous, loyal and disciplined, while holding rules that another group experiences as unjust or dangerous.

The book's second major contribution is the distinction between immediate moral reaction and deliberate comparison. A person's first response may reveal their social commitments, but it does not automatically settle the issue. Deliberation can expose biased fairness, selective empathy and the costs hidden by group loyalty.

Greene's answer, deep pragmatism, is a serious philosophical proposal rather than a proven scientific conclusion. We should borrow the research questions and the conflict architecture without turning every character into a utilitarian or every conflict into a numerical calculation.

**Most important principle:** Build groups whose internal rules make sense to their members, then place those rules in conflict over something both groups need or care about.

**Canon status:** Research only. No story decisions approved. No new mythology. No causal links inferred.
