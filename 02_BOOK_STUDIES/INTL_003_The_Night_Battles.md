## 1. Source identity, structure and method

The supplied edition identifies the work as a study of witchcraft and agrarian cults in sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Friuli. Its original Italian title is *I Benandanti*, first published in 1966. The English translation is credited to John and Anne Tedeschi. The publisher's front matter states that the English translation first appeared in 1983 and that the supplied Routledge Library Editions reprint is from 2011.

The translators' note records an important textual limitation. The English version reproduces the 1974 second Italian edition, with one paragraph deleted and only a handful of new bibliographical references added. The translators also explain that the trial notaries translated testimony from Friulian into Italian, changed direct testimony into third-person indirect discourse and converted question-and-answer exchanges into indirect narration. The translators retain the source's inconsistent personal-name forms where the original records are inconsistent. They use *benandante* and *benandanti* as the Italian terms for the people studied, glossing the word as approximately "those who go well" or "good-doers".

The book contains four main chapters:

1. **The Night Battles** — printed pp. 1-32
2. **The Processions of the Dead** — printed pp. 33-68
3. **The Benandanti between Inquisitors and Witches** — printed pp. 69-98
4. **The Benandanti at the Sabbat** — printed pp. 99-146

These are followed by an appendix of archival records (pp. 147-172), notes (pp. 173-204) and an index of names (beginning p. 205).

This is historical microhistory, not a conventional folklore catalogue and not a supernatural narrative. Ginzburg reconstructs a local field of belief through a sequence of individual depositions, accusations, interrogations, sentences, correspondence and later cases. He deliberately gives attention to named people and their changing circumstances instead of treating the peasantry as a single, uniform mentality.

## 2. The central argument, carefully stated

The book is often summarized as the discovery of a hidden fertility cult that was transformed into witchcraft by the Inquisition. That is too simple.

Ginzburg's narrower and more defensible argument is that, in Friuli from the late sixteenth century onward, a distinct complex of popular beliefs existed around people called *benandanti*. In their earlier accounts, they described themselves as defenders of crops, families, children and household provisions against harmful witches. They said they travelled at night in spirit, often while their bodies remained inert in bed, and fought adversaries with fennel stalks. The adversaries used sorghum or other field stalks. The outcome of the struggle was connected to the coming harvest.

These statements did not initially fit the learned demonological description of the witch's sabbat. The benandanti did not initially describe worship of the Devil, repudiation of Christianity, desecration of sacred objects or the full ritual system expected by inquisitors. They often insisted that they were Christians and that their work protected the faith and the community.

Over several decades, this distinction became blurred. Inquisitors asked questions framed by their own demonological categories. Witnesses, neighbours, clergy, healers and accused people began repeating or adapting the same vocabulary. Some benandanti resisted the identification with witches. Others accepted parts of it, used it in accusations, or described increasingly sabbat-like gatherings. Their self-descriptions changed, but not uniformly or in a straight line.

**Ginzburg does not claim that the surviving documents prove that every reported night gathering physically took place as described.** In the English preface he explicitly says that no document establishes with certainty that benandanti met at fixed places and times to perform the reported rites. The available evidence supports the existence of a meaningful and socially shared belief complex. Whether the nocturnal battles occurred as physical gatherings, dreams, trances, visions or some combination cannot be resolved from these records alone.

The book's importance lies partly in this unresolved boundary. The historian can reconstruct what people believed, how those beliefs shaped behaviour and how their language changed, without claiming that a supernatural explanation has been verified.

## 3. The benandanti belief complex

The early descriptions contain several recurring elements. They do not all appear in every deposition, and the book does not treat every person as having an identical experience.

| Element | How it appears in testimony | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| Birth with the caul | Some people say they were born with the membrane covering the head and that this marked or compelled them to become benandanti. | A reported local sign and belief, not evidence of supernatural destiny. |
| Compulsion or calling | Some say they cannot refuse the night journey. The calling begins at a particular age or is announced through a summons, sometimes described through drum-like imagery. | Testimony about how participants understood their role. |
| Spirit journey | The person says the spirit goes out while the body remains asleep, inert or apparently dead. | Reported experience. The records do not independently verify the separation of spirit and body. |
| Seasonal timing | Ember Days, Thursdays and other feast days recur in accounts. | A patterned feature of testimony, with variation between cases. |
| Night battle | The benandanti fight those described as harmful witches or warlocks. | A central reported ritual image, not proof of physical combat. |
| Plant weapons | Benandanti carry fennel, while opponents carry sorghum or other stalks. | A recurring symbolic distinction in the records. |
| Agricultural outcome | The struggle is connected with abundance or scarcity in the coming harvest. | The core agrarian meaning attributed to the practice. |
| Protection and healing | Benandanti say they prevent harm to children, livestock, food stores, wine and crops, and sometimes cure people believed to be bewitched. | A social role claimed by participants and recognized by some neighbours. |
| Secrecy and danger | Participants say that disclosure can bring punishment from the opposing company. | A belief used to explain silence and the difficulty of obtaining testimony. |

The *caul* is important because it is treated as a material sign of an invisible calling. Some accounts describe mothers preserving the membrane, sometimes with religious observances around it. The belief can pass through family stories. A person may be told from childhood that the sign determines a future role.

This gives the benandanti identity a social and familial dimension. It is not merely an individual's private dream. The role can be known, discussed, feared, mocked, sought for help or used in disputes.

## 4. Chapter-by-chapter study

### Chapter I — The Night Battles (printed pp. 1-32)

The opening case begins on 21 March 1575 at the monastery of San Francesco in Cividale. A local priest, Don Bartolomeo Sgabarizza, reports what he has heard from Paolo Gasparutto of Iassico. Gasparutto had told the father of a sick child that the child was in danger from witches but had been protected by a group he called benandanti. He also claimed that he could provide a remedy.

Gasparutto's account describes night journeys, especially around the Ember Days, and battles in which the benandanti use fennel stalks against adversaries who carry sorghum stalks. The company travels between local places and sometimes over considerable distances. The adversaries are blamed for harming children, entering houses and damaging wine or provisions. The benandanti claim to prevent these harms.

The priest's reports bring in a second figure, Battista Moduco, a public crier in Cividale, who also describes himself as a benandante. The early ecclesiastical response is uncertain and somewhat dismissive. Some witnesses call them witches, while the benandanti insist that they are the good or protective company. The contradiction is present from the beginning.

The enquiry resumes under a new inquisitor, Fra Felice da Montefalco, in June 1580. Gasparutto initially denies knowledge, laughs at the questions and tries to withdraw earlier statements. Under persistent questioning he gives a more detailed account of the spirit journey and the conditions attached to it. Moduco is questioned separately. Their testimony brings out the role of the caul, the apparent inability to refuse the calling and the danger said to arise if the body is disturbed while the spirit is away.

The distinction from the classical sabbat matters. The early benandanti accounts do not centre on the Devil or a renunciation of Christianity. Their reported combat is framed as defence of crops and people. Some also describe their actions as defending the Christian faith. Ginzburg interprets this as a possible Christian adaptation of older agrarian symbolism, but he does not treat every element as a transparent survival from antiquity.

The chapter then places the Friulian material beside other European evidence, including accounts of seasonal struggles and the later Livonian werewolf trial of Thiess. Thiess describes werewolves as defenders of fields and livestock against witches. Ginzburg considers the similarity significant and proposes possible links with a wider complex of agrarian and nocturnal beliefs. He also raises possible connections with shamanic traditions, especially spirit travel and animal forms. These are comparative hypotheses, not proof of a universal European cult or of a single origin.

The first cases end with sentences against Gasparutto and Moduco in 1581. The punishments are limited compared with the later image of witch persecution. The inquisitorial record nevertheless changes the terms in which their identity is described.

**Chapter lesson:** A community role that participants describe as protective can be placed inside a hostile institutional category before the institution fully understands what the participants mean.

### Chapter II — The Processions of the Dead (printed pp. 33-68)

The second chapter widens the inquiry beyond the night battles. Ginzburg examines people who say they visit, see or speak with the dead. The connection to the benandanti is not assumed in advance. It is built through recurring testimony about the caul, trance-like states, a spirit leaving the body and the danger of revealing what happened during the absence.

Anna la Rossa is one of the central cases. She is known for speaking about the dead and sometimes uses these claims to obtain food or small payments from people who are grieving. She describes journeys in which the body remains as if dead while the spirit travels. She also speaks of the dead returning to their homes in an exhausted condition and of the need for beds to be prepared for them on particular nights.

Other depositions connect the ability to encounter the dead with birth under a caul. Some witnesses describe a relative or spouse whose body could not be roused during a journey. Others say that those who return from the world of the dead are reluctant to reveal what they have seen because of the threat of punishment.

Ginzburg is careful with the relationship between these reports and the benandanti. Anna is not initially identified by that name. The evidence is scattered. He argues that the recurring features suggest a relationship, but he does not pretend that every testimony belongs to one completely unified institution.

The chapter then follows related testimony from other women and men, including accounts that invoke a field or meadow, heavenly or otherworldly spaces, flowers, processions and the presence of dead relatives. Some people use these stories to provide consolation. Others use them for money or social leverage. The records include embellishment, contradiction and possible invention.

The comparison with the Wild Hunt, Diana, Holda, Perchta and processions of the dead in medieval European sources is important but qualified. These traditions have overlapping motifs, such as nocturnal travel and companies of the dead, but they are not identical. The dead can be frightening or morally instructive in one tradition, while the Friulian reports sometimes emphasize family ties, restoration, warning and the care of the living.

The chapter also shows how belief can become a social service and a social liability. A person who claims to speak with the dead may be consulted by grieving families, paid for information, feared as a witch or accused of deception. The same practice can have different meanings depending on who is speaking and what they need.

**Chapter lesson:** Similar images do not automatically establish one shared ritual. The historian must track who used the image, in which context, with what purpose and with what degree of certainty.

### Chapter III — The Benandanti between Inquisitors and Witches (printed pp. 69-98)

The third chapter follows the spread and alteration of benandanti testimony across the Friuli. A series of local cases shows that the belief does not remain confined to the first two men.

Toffolo di Buri, a shepherd from the Monfalcone area, describes years of compelled night journeys and struggles with field stalks. The familiar harvest-protection structure appears again, but the details differ. The inquisitors do not always pursue these cases with the same intensity. In some places, the alleged activity is treated as superstition rather than a clear heresy.

A case involving the midwife Caterina Domenatta records a practice applied to a child born feet first. The child is reportedly turned over a fire on a spit three times to prevent the child from becoming a benandante or witch. The episode reveals how the caul and the benandante destiny can become part of local fears about birth. It is evidence of a reported custom and belief, not proof that the child would actually acquire a supernatural identity.

Menichino della Nota, a cowherd from Latisana, gives a detailed account of visiting a field of Josaphat in a dream-like or spirit state. He describes seasonal timing, flowers, mist, a journey over water and fighting with fennel stalks. He says that he was told he was a benandante and that he would have to go, even though he did not want to. His account shows how the identity can be passed to a person through the words of others and then incorporated into a remembered dream.

The chapter moves from the ritual claim into the local social world. Some benandanti present themselves as healers who can identify witches or remove their effects. Others accuse neighbours of harming children, livestock or harvests. Their claims produce rivalry, fear, family disputes and reputational damage.

Lunardo Badau and Gerolamo Cut are important examples of this social escalation. Their public claims that particular women are witches create conflict and distress. People can begin to call the accuser a witch or fraud in return. The category is no longer simply an inquisitorial label. It circulates in village disputes and can be used by neighbours against one another.

Ginzburg also contrasts the popular understanding of compulsion with the educated view of free will. The idea that a benandante cannot refuse the calling is difficult for educated interrogators to accept. This difference is not merely theological. It reveals different assumptions about responsibility, destiny, the body and the limits of a person's control.

The chapter further notes competition between healers and exorcists, and the political tension between ecclesiastical tribunals and Venetian secular authority. An inquisitor may ask questions, threaten action or seek a confession, but a case can stall when the alleged practice does not clearly meet the tribunal's definition of heresy.

**Chapter lesson:** A supernatural label can become a weapon in ordinary social life. The consequences of a belief do not depend on whether the reported supernatural event can be independently verified.

### Chapter IV — The Benandanti at the Sabbat (printed pp. 99-146)

The final chapter follows later testimony in which the language of the traditional sabbat becomes more prominent. The older benandanti distinction does not disappear immediately, but it becomes increasingly difficult to preserve.

Maria Panzona is an important example of contradictory self-description. In one phase of her case she describes herself in ways that resemble a sabbat attendee. In other accounts she returns to the older claim that benandanti are different from witches and perform a protective role. She changes or withdraws statements as the questioning changes. Her record shows that testimony can contain competing explanations within the same person's account.

Giovanni Sion's case contains a more elaborate diabolical narrative. His testimony describes gatherings, travel, feasting and a devil figure, and is closer to the learned demonological template. Yet the account remains contradictory in places, and not all people named or accused confirm his claims. The tribunal's questions and the witness's expanding descriptions interact. Ginzburg tracks how the testimony moves toward a recognizable sabbat pattern without treating every detail as independently verified.

Michele Soppe, a healer active in the mid-seventeenth century, provides another view of the changing status of the benandanti. He has a public reputation, charges for services and becomes involved in disputes with neighbours and religious authorities. In his later account, he describes gatherings with more familiar sabbat elements, while traces of the older distinction between benandanti and witches remain. The old vocabulary persists as a kind of residue inside a substantially altered story.

Olivo Caldo's case shows how a confession can be assembled under pressure. He gives statements that fit the interrogator's expectations, then retracts or modifies them. When the pressure shifts, the details become unstable. Ginzburg treats this instability as evidence about the production of a confession, not merely as proof that the witness is lying.

Other testimony shows how accusations and confessions can be shaped by emotional need, domestic conflict, social exclusion and the desire to explain suffering. Ginzburg does not reduce every case to inquisitorial coercion. Some people may find attraction, pleasure, meaning or a language for distress in the sabbat image. The process is therefore not a simple one-way imposition from educated judges onto passive peasants.

The chapter's final question is whether the reported gatherings physically happened. Ginzburg argues that the documents cannot settle this. Witnesses and accusers inhabit a shared field of belief and expectation. Their accounts are historically consequential even when they cannot establish the physical reality of the events they describe.

**Chapter lesson:** A later, standardized account should not be projected backward onto earlier testimony. When language changes over decades, the change itself is historical evidence.

## 6. Evidence architecture: how to read the records

The book's method is as important as its subject. A useful way to audit each historical claim is to separate five layers:

1. **The reported event or experience:** what the person says happened.
2. **The witness chain:** whether the speaker experienced it, heard it directly from someone else, or repeated local talk.
3. **The interrogator's framing:** what the judge asks, assumes, suggests or supplies.
4. **The notarial record:** how speech was translated, condensed, reordered or converted into indirect narration.
5. **The historian's inference:** what Ginzburg argues the record supports, and what he says remains unproven.

This distinction prevents several common errors:

- A person describing a spirit journey does not prove that a spirit physically left the body.
- A witness repeating another person's description is not an independent eyewitness.
- A confession that adopts the vocabulary of a sabbat does not prove that the earlier benandanti practice had always been a sabbat.
- An inquisitor's question is evidence of the court's assumptions, not evidence that the assumed event occurred.
- A contradiction does not automatically make the entire testimony useless. It may show the effect of pressure, memory, fear, changing identity or the teller's own interests.

The translators' note is especially important here. The testimony is valuable, but the modern reader does not receive an unmediated transcript of a Friulian peasant speaking directly. Notaries had already changed its form.

## 7. The book's major conceptual distinctions

### 7.1 Popular belief versus learned demonology

The early benandanti accounts are not simply a peasant version of the learned sabbat. The benandanti describe a protective agricultural struggle. The inquisitors approach the subject with an established framework of diabolical witchcraft. The conflict between those conceptual systems drives much of the record.

### 7.2 Belief versus physical verification

A belief can shape work, reputation, family behaviour, accusations and fear even when the reported event cannot be established as a physical occurrence. Ginzburg's historical task is to reconstruct the belief system and its consequences, not to decide the supernatural question by assertion.

### 7.3 Shared tradition versus uniform cult

The book identifies recurring motifs across cases, but the people do not all describe the same experience in the same way. The historian must resist turning a constellation of similarities into an overly neat institution with a single doctrine and fixed ritual.

### 7.4 Imposition versus internalization

The categories of the court do not remain outside the community. They enter gossip, rivalry, accusation and the self-descriptions of later witnesses. The process is interactive. Institutional pressure matters, but so do local interests and the choices of individuals.

### 7.5 Continuity versus transformation

Some elements remain recognizable over generations, including caul, nocturnal journey, field, stalks and seasonal timing. Other details change as sabbat language becomes more prominent. The record shows neither total continuity nor a single moment of invention.

## 8. Visual evidence: the illustrations of the Furious Horde

The book includes an instructive group of illustrations in the section on the procession and the Furious Horde (printed pp. 45-47).

The first illustration, from Geiler von Kaisersberg's *Die Emeis* (1516), uses an image of Bacchus and his followers to represent a subject for which the illustrator apparently lacked a suitable visual model. The later image replaces that borrowed scene with a fool's wagon derived from Sebastian Brant's *Stultifera navis*. The sequence is significant because it shows how a learned visual language can substitute for a folk belief that is difficult to depict directly.

The images do not function as straightforward documentary pictures of the reported procession. They demonstrate the problem of representation: when a cultural form has no established image, an author or illustrator may borrow an available motif from elite literature and make it stand in for something else.

**Craft relevance:** The images are evidence of mediation, not evidence that the illustrated event happened. A visual record can show how a belief was represented by someone outside the community rather than what the community itself saw.

## 9. Strengths and limitations

### Strengths

- The book reconstructs change over time through named individuals rather than reducing the evidence to an abstract collective mentality.
- It distinguishes the initial benandanti claims from the demonological categories applied by interrogators.
- It follows how belief enters family life, local healing, public reputation and village conflict.
- It uses contradictions and changes in testimony as evidence about historical processes.
- It is unusually explicit about the limits of what the records can prove.
- The appendix makes the early trial documents available for the reader to compare with the author's interpretation.
- It gives a strong model for treating records as produced objects with witnesses, interrogators, notaries and later editors.

### Limitations and cautions

- The evidence is geographically and historically specific to Friuli. It cannot be generalized automatically to other European regions or to Tamil folklore.
- Many statements are not direct eyewitness accounts. Some are hearsay, repetition or testimony prompted by questioning.
- The notarial transformation from Friulian to Italian and from direct to indirect discourse complicates any claim that the peasant voice reaches the modern reader without mediation.
- The evidence does not establish that all reported nocturnal gatherings physically occurred.
- Ginzburg's proposals about a widespread ancient agrarian cult and links to shamanism are comparative hypotheses. They should not be repeated as proven historical facts.
- The supplied English edition's apparatus was not fully updated after the 1974 Italian edition, according to the translators' note. The book should be treated as a historically important study, not as a fully current survey of scholarship.
- The trial materials come from an institution investigating suspected heresy. The archive's survival itself reflects the priorities and categories of that institution.

## 10. Transferable research and storytelling techniques

The most useful lessons are methodological and structural. They are not instructions to import Friulian beliefs into a Tamil setting.

1. **Build a claim ladder.** Separate direct observation, hearsay, local explanation, accusation, official interpretation and later reconstruction.
2. **Track the questioner.** Record what the investigator asks before interpreting the answer. A leading question can shape the evidence that follows.
3. **Preserve the witness's own vocabulary.** Do not replace a character's term with the investigator's category before establishing the difference.
4. **Record changes in language over time.** A belief may be altered through repeated retelling, pressure, social fashion or the need to explain a new experience.
5. **Keep multiple local models alive.** Different people can explain the same occurrence through different systems without the story needing to declare all of them equally true.
6. **Use contradictions diagnostically.** A contradiction can expose pressure, self-protection, memory failure, conflicting loyalties or a changed self-understanding.
7. **Follow the social consequences of belief.** A claim can produce economic exchange, stigma, protection, conflict, loyalty and punishment whether or not the underlying supernatural event is verifiable.
8. **Separate the event from its record.** A report is created by someone, for a purpose, through an institutional or social process.
9. **Do not force a single explanatory answer.** The historical record may support a confident account of belief and social change while leaving physical occurrence unsettled.
10. **Treat visual representation as another layer of mediation.** An illustration, diagram or ritual prop may reveal the representer's categories rather than the original experience.

## 13. Study verdict

**Strongest lesson:** An investigation must preserve the difference between what happened, what a witness says happened, what a community believes happened, what an investigator expects to hear and what the surviving record can actually establish.

**Most important historical insight:** The benandanti were not simply witches under another name. The early testimony describes a locally meaningful protective identity tied to harvests, the caul, spirit journeys and community welfare. Across decades, that identity was increasingly entangled with demonological witchcraft, but the transformation was incomplete, contested and affected by both institutional pressure and local social uses of accusation.

**Biggest warning:** Do not turn Ginzburg's argument into either of two certainties: that a fully organized ancient fertility cult is proven, or that inquisitors invented every element of the belief. The book demonstrates a historically specific belief complex and a process of changing interpretation. It leaves the physical reality of the reported night gatherings unresolved.
