## 0. Research question and executive finding

### Research question

**What changes when the dead return inside a community whose religious institutions, domestic obligations and shared stories already provide explanations for the supernatural?**

The films are separated by thirty-two years, national cinema, production system, cultural setting and emotional register. Yet both organise horror around a dead young woman whose presence unsettles the living, and around a male protagonist whose response is shaped by social and religious authority.

The resemblance should not erase the differences.

- *Viy* is a Soviet adaptation of Nikolai Gogol's Ukrainian-set literary horror story. A seminarian, Khoma Brutus, is forced to keep vigil and pray over the body of a young woman. Across three nights, the film turns a religious duty into an escalating contest between prayer, fear, ritual space and a supernatural adversary.
- *Nang Nak* reworks the well-known Thai Mae Nak / Mae Nak Phra Khanong legend. Mak returns from war to a home that appears intact. The wife and child he embraces are dead, but his attachment and the community's uneven knowledge delay recognition. The film's emotional centre is not simply the exposure of a ghost. It is the collision between love, denial, grief, Buddhist religious authority and the social consequences of a haunting.

**Pair finding:** *Viy* makes ritual protection a timed ordeal, where the protagonist's ability to hold a boundary is tested over successive nights. *Nang Nak* makes domestic attachment the boundary problem, where the desire to preserve a marriage prevents the living from recognising death. In one, religious duty is an explicit defensive procedure. In the other, religious authority eventually becomes the means through which attachment is released.

A second finding is methodological. Neither film should be treated as a transparent ethnographic record. *Viy* sits between Gogol's literary invention and documented Ukrainian folkloric parallels, a question on which scholarship remains contested. *Nang Nak* is one influential modern cinematic version of a legend that has been repeatedly retold, politically reinterpreted and commercially remade.

## 2. Cultural, literary and historical boundaries

### 2.1 Viy: Gogol, Ukrainian setting and disputed folklore provenance

**SOURCE**
Gogol's story presents Viy as a supernatural being whose enormous eyelids must be lifted so that he can see. The story's narrator frames Viy as a creation of the imagination of ordinary people and claims to relay a popular legend without alteration. The reliability of that framing is itself disputed.

The available scholarship does not support a simple yes/no statement:
- BFI's programme essay, drawing on Sight & Sound criticism, notes that Gogol's supposed folk source may be a literary invention. It points out that the named figure Viy itself is difficult to locate in the historical folklore record.
- A 2024 peer-reviewed article by Kostyantyn Rakhno, “Viy in Nikolai Gogol’s Novella and Related Mythological Creatures in Ukrainian Folklore,” argues that there are Ukrainian folkloric parallels: demonic beings with different names but similar appearance and functions, including the eye / eyelid motif.
- A 2014 study of Soviet and post-Soviet horror describes *Viy* as based on Ukrainian folklore and treats the film as a foundational Soviet horror work. That broad description should be read alongside the more specific provenance debate rather than as a final resolution.

**ANALYSIS**
The careful formulation is:
1. Gogol locates the story in a Ukrainian cultural landscape and frames it as a popular legend.
2. The named entity “Viy” may be Gogol's literary construction, or a literary crystallisation of a wider cluster of folk motifs.
3. Related motifs and beings in Ukrainian folklore have been documented and analysed.
4. The film adapts Gogol's story, not a directly documented ritual or a single verified oral account.
5. The film's religious and supernatural world is therefore mediated through literature, Soviet cinema and the filmmakers' design.

**CULTURAL BOUNDARY**
Do not describe the film as an accurate documentary of Ukrainian folk religion. Do not say that all Ukrainian communities historically believed in a single entity called Viy with exactly Gogol's features. Equally, do not erase Ukrainian folkloric parallels by declaring the creature wholly invented. The evidence supports a contested relationship between a literary name / character and a wider motif-family.

### 2.2 Nang Nak: Mae Nak as legend, cultural memory and repeated adaptation

**SOURCE**
Mae Nak Phra Khanong is a widely known Thai ghost legend. In the common narrative pattern, Nak dies during childbirth while her husband Mak is away at war. Mak returns and lives with his wife and child without realising they are dead. The community knows more than he does. The legend has been retold in numerous film, theatrical and popular forms.

Scholarship identifies *Nang Nak* (1999) as a major turning point in the modern cinematic life of the story:
- Arnika Fuhrmann's 2009 article reads the film through desire, embodiment, temporal incongruity and Buddhist melancholia. Her account foregrounds the film's presentation of a woman whose desire persists beyond death and whose husband cannot initially share her knowledge of time and mortality.
- May Adadol Ingawanij's 2007 article analyses *Nang Nak* as Thai bourgeois heritage cinema. She argues that its nostalgic, carefully designed past can function as a self-exoticising image of “Old Siam,” particularly in the context of national self-representation and international festival circulation.
- Hock Soon Ng's 2014 article compares cinematic versions of Mae Nak and argues that Nak's representation shifts with social and ideological context. In the 1999 version, the figure is restored to a powerful and culturally resonant position.
- Adam Knee's 2014 study of later Mae Nak remakes treats the 1999 film as a turning point in the contemporary cinematic history of the icon.
- Mary Jane Ainslie's 2023 book chapter situates the film in the growth of New Thai Cinema, heritage representation and the economic / ideological context of the late 1990s.

**ANALYSIS**
The legend is not a fixed text. Each retelling chooses how to distribute sympathy, fear, religious authority, gendered duty and national memory.
- Nak may be framed as a dangerous ghost, a wronged wife, a tragic lover, a powerful supernatural figure, or several of these at once.
- Mak's ignorance can make him an object of pathos, a figure of denial, or a person whose attachment becomes ethically costly.
- The community may function as a witness network, a source of warning, a moral authority or an obstacle.
- The monk's intervention can be framed as compassion, spiritual expertise, social restoration or a means of disciplining an unruly female ghost.
- The film's nineteenth-century setting is a cinematic reconstruction, not proof that every costume, ritual or domestic detail is historically exact.

**CULTURAL BOUNDARY**
Do not treat *Nang Nak* as a transparent record of Thai Buddhist practice, historical childbirth, village organisation or nineteenth-century life. The film is a contemporary adaptation of a living legend and a heritage production. The historical setting, visual beauty and religious material are artistic and ideological choices that require independent historical evidence if treated as documentary claims.

### 2.3 Buddhist ritual and religious authority: avoid flattening

The two films do not depict interchangeable religious systems.
- *Viy* draws on a Christian seminary, prayer, sacred space and a ritual vigil within Gogol's literary world. Its cinematic conflict is shaped by a particular Orthodox Christian-coded setting, but the film is not a general account of Eastern Christian practice.
- *Nang Nak* uses Thai Buddhist monastic authority and the social presence of the temple. The film's resolution is linked to a senior monk's spiritual intervention and Mak's subsequent religious response. This is not the same ritual system as *Viy*.

The comparison should focus on the narrative function of religious authority, not collapse the religions into generic “exorcism.”

## 3. Viy: narrative architecture and scene progression

### 3.1 The road, the lost students and the comic baseline

**SOURCE**
The film begins with seminarians travelling home for vacation. Their journey becomes disorganised; they lose their route and seek shelter in a remote settlement. The early tone includes comic behaviour, appetite, bravado and institutional discipline.

**ANALYSIS**
The opening matters because it establishes Khoma as a person before the supernatural ordeal:
- He is not introduced as a pure spiritual hero.
- His appetite, humour and physicality make him ordinary and fallible.
- The seminary is a social institution with rules, but the students are capable of evading them.
- The journey removes the students from familiar institutional protection and places them in an unfamiliar household / rural environment.
- The comic register lowers the audience's expectation of solemn supernatural seriousness.

The film's later horror is more effective because the protagonist begins as a recognisable, imperfect young man rather than an already prepared exorcist.

### 3.2 The old woman and the first impossible encounter

The encounter with the old woman and the strange night ride marks the transition from comic travel narrative to supernatural ordeal. Khoma experiences an event that exceeds his ordinary explanation of the world. The film's witch figure moves between vulnerable appearance and terrifying agency.

**ANALYSIS**
The sequence establishes an important asymmetry:
- Khoma is physically present but epistemically unprepared.
- The apparent victim has power over the encounter.
- The body becomes the site of uncertainty: what looks like an old woman is not simply what it seems.
- The encounter is not explained through a long exposition scene. It is experienced through movement, fear and aftermath.

The film does not need to establish a complex mythology before the audience feels the violation. It first establishes that the ordinary categories Khoma uses are unreliable.

### 3.3 The demand to perform the vigil

Khoma is ordered to spend three nights praying over the dead young woman. The assignment is imposed through social authority and material power. He is not freely choosing a spiritual mission.

**ANALYSIS**
The structure converts a supernatural threat into a procedural obligation:
1. A death has occurred.
2. The family / household has power to demand a religious service.
3. Khoma is selected and transported into the obligation.
4. The space and timing are fixed.
5. The vigil repeats across three nights.
6. Each repetition increases anticipation and changes the threat.

The horror has a clock. The audience understands the basic task and can anticipate the next stage without knowing what will happen.

### 3.4 First night: the boundary is tested

The first night establishes the ritual chamber and its defensive logic. Khoma prays within a bounded space while the supernatural presence attempts to reach him.

**ANALYSIS**
The circle / sacred boundary functions as both physical geography and mental support:
- It gives Khoma a clear action to perform.
- It provides the audience with a visible rule.
- It makes the room legible as a temporary refuge.
- The threat becomes more frightening because the protective space is explicit.
- Khoma's fear is not merely fear of a monster. It is fear that the procedure he depends on may fail.

The sequence makes ritual visible. Prayer is not only dialogue or belief. It becomes a timed action performed inside a designed spatial boundary.

### 3.5 Second night: escalation through repetition

The second night repeats the basic premise but alters the intensity and scale of the supernatural assault.

**ANALYSIS**
Repetition works because the viewer now knows the procedure:
- The first night teaches the rule.
- The second night tests whether the rule is sufficient.
- The audience recognises the room, the task and the vulnerability.
- The change in threat is therefore measurable against the previous encounter.
- Khoma's psychological deterioration becomes part of the suspense.

The film uses recurrence rather than constant novelty. A repeated ritual becomes more frightening when each return changes the cost.

### 3.6 Third night: the named adversary and the final failure

The final night brings the story's supernatural hierarchy into focus with the arrival of Viy. The creature's ability to see what is hidden transforms the earlier contest. Khoma's protection depends on not looking, not crossing the boundary and maintaining the prayer procedure.

**ANALYSIS**
The final sequence is built from a chain of procedural failure:
- Khoma's fear becomes harder to contain.
- The supernatural threat is given a distinct form.
- The audience has been prepared for a final escalation through the two earlier nights.
- The instruction not to look becomes the decisive rule.
- The moment the rule is broken, the protected space loses its power.

The film's climax is not simply “a monster appears.” It is the collapse of a rule the audience has learned and watched the protagonist struggle to maintain.

### 3.7 Ending and consequence

The ending closes the three-night ordeal with fatal consequence. The story's moral and religious atmosphere remains more complex than a simple equation of weak faith and punishment.

**ANALYSIS**
Khoma's defeat can be read through multiple overlapping factors:
- He is frightened and physically exhausted.
- He has been forced into a task beyond his capacity.
- His earlier comic irreverence and appetites are not erased by the ordeal.
- The ritual boundary is real within the film's world, but the human being inside it is vulnerable.
- The supernatural event exposes the gap between institutional assignment and individual capacity.

**OPEN / NEEDS VERIFICATION**
A shot-by-shot account of the final apparition, camera placement and exact visual effects timing requires an edition-matched viewing pass. The dossier records the narrative mechanism, not a frame-accurate breakdown.

## 4. Viy: mystery, evidence and psychology

### 4.1 What is the central mystery?

The film is not a whodunit. Its questions are:
- What is the young woman?
- What happened during Khoma's first encounter?
- Why has Khoma been selected for the vigil?
- What are the limits of the protective ritual?
- Can Khoma survive three nights inside a space where the dead are active?
- What is Viy's particular power, and what condition allows that power to operate?

The audience's knowledge grows through repeated encounters rather than through detective reconstruction.

### 4.2 Evidence chain

| Stage | Observable evidence | Khoma / community interpretation | Dramatic effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lost journey | Students cannot find the route and seek shelter | Ordinary travel trouble | Moves characters outside familiar protection |
| First encounter | Impossible ride / transformation and bodily aftermath | Khoma confronts a witch-like power | Breaks ordinary categories |
| Death and request | Young woman dies and Khoma is assigned a vigil | Religious service is required | Converts encounter into duty |
| First night | Disturbances occur inside a bounded room | Prayer and boundary protect him | Establishes the rule |
| Second night | Stronger assault and repeated threat | The protection is under pressure | Escalates through recurrence |
| Third night | Viy is summoned; gaze becomes decisive | The final threat exceeds Khoma's control | Turns a rule into a fatal test |

### 4.3 Khoma's psychology

Khoma is not a detached investigator. He is a young seminarian who moves between appetite, humour, fear and institutional obligation.
- **Appetite:** the opening presents bodily need and comic indulgence.
- **Bravado:** he is capable of acting confidently when the world seems ordinary.
- **Fear:** the first encounter makes his body respond before he has a stable explanation.
- **Avoidance:** he does not seek the ordeal as a test of faith.
- **Exhaustion:** the three-night structure places cumulative pressure on his capacity.
- **Rule dependence:** the ritual provides a procedural support that is both spiritual and psychological.

The film's horror is strongest when it does not reduce him to cowardice. He is placed in a situation where the task, the institution and the supernatural threat are radically mismatched.

### 4.4 Authority and coercion

The vigil is a religious duty, but it is also an imposed assignment backed by a powerful household. The film therefore contains two forms of authority:
- institutional / religious authority that defines the service;
- local / social power that can compel Khoma to perform it.

The distinction matters. Khoma's participation is not a clean expression of voluntary belief. His agency is constrained.

### 4.5 Ritual as procedure, not magic exposition

The film does not need a lecture on ritual mechanics. It teaches the viewer through repeated performance:
- where Khoma stands;
- what he must do;
- what boundary he relies on;
- what he must not do;
- what happens when the procedure fails.

This is a legible dramatic system. The ritual's force is conveyed through action and consequence.

## 5. Viy: visual, spatial and sonic grammar

### 5.1 Space and enclosure

The film moves from roads and rural exteriors into the vigil chamber. This spatial contraction supports the narrative:
- the journey begins with movement and uncertain direction;
- the household offers shelter but also entrapment;
- the ritual room becomes the arena;
- the circle / bounded space gives the audience a simple map of safety;
- the supernatural threat becomes more intense as the room's protective function is tested.

The chamber is not merely a set. It is a rule made visible.

### 5.2 Effects and creature design

Aleksandr Ptushko's special-effects role is central to the film's reputation. The effects do not depend on modern photorealism. Their force comes from scale, grotesque physicality, movement, creature variety and the collision between theatrical artifice and the ritual chamber.

**ANALYSIS**
The effects create a bestiary rather than a single generic apparition. That variety makes the supernatural world feel populated, but it also risks episodic spectacle if the audience loses sight of Khoma's psychological ordeal. The three-night structure provides the continuity that holds the effects together.

### 5.3 Colour and production design

The film's colour world and rural architecture create a folkloric atmosphere without pretending to be documentary realism. Its village, church / seminary and ritual interiors operate as stylised story spaces.

### 5.4 Sound and music

The score is credited to Karen Khachaturyan. Available institutional and production records establish the composer and sound credits, but this pass did not locate a technical study of the soundtrack's recording, mixing or cue-by-cue design.

**ANALYSIS**
Sound should be studied in relation to:
- the shift from comic travel to supernatural unease;
- the acoustic difference between outdoor movement and enclosed ritual;
- the way silence and sudden sound can punctuate the three-night structure;
- the relationship between music, creature movement and the viewer's anticipation.

Do not attribute a particular sound cue, instrument or recording technique to the production without direct evidence.

## 6. Nang Nak: narrative architecture and scene progression

### 6.1 The separation: Mak goes to war

The film establishes Nak and Mak's domestic relationship before Mak's absence. War separates the couple. Nak is pregnant, and the household becomes a place of waiting.

**ANALYSIS**
The war is not only historical background. It creates the temporal gap on which the ghost story depends:
- Mak leaves while Nak is alive.
- The childbirth and death occur during his absence.
- Mak returns with the expectation of continuity.
- Nak's waiting has become impossible because she is dead.
- The couple's emotional time has diverged.

The horror grows from an ordinary social fact: war separates families and delays information.

### 6.2 Return home: the domestic idyll

Mak returns and appears to resume married life. Nak and the child are present. The house, routines and affection suggest that the separation has ended.

**ANALYSIS**
The film's central dramatic advantage is that the ghost is not initially staged as an external attacker. She occupies the domestic centre:
- the home is not immediately frightening;
- familiar routines reassure Mak;
- affection is sincere within the film's emotional logic;
- the audience gradually recognises that the ordinary scene contains an impossible fact;
- the domestic setting becomes the source of dread because it refuses to look like a haunted house.

The supernatural is hidden inside continuity rather than announced through spectacle.

### 6.3 Anomalies and community knowledge

Neighbours and other villagers observe signs that Mak does not understand. The community's knowledge accumulates unevenly.

**ANALYSIS**
This creates an information asymmetry:
- Nak knows she is dead.
- Mak believes she is alive.
- Some villagers know or suspect the truth.
- Others respond through fear, gossip, avoidance or warning.
- The audience is positioned between intimacy with the couple and growing access to the community's knowledge.

The village is not a single chorus with one shared belief. It is a network of partial witnesses.

### 6.4 The revelation: ordinary appearance reclassified

The truth becomes increasingly difficult to conceal. The film's reveal reclassifies earlier domestic details.

**ANALYSIS**
The reveal has retrospective force:
- Earlier affection becomes tragic rather than reassuring.
- The child is no longer simply a sign of family continuity.
- The house becomes a space where life and death have been made to coexist.
- The villagers' fear becomes more intelligible.
- Mak's ignorance becomes emotionally painful rather than merely foolish.

The most effective clue design here is not necessarily a hidden object. It is a change in interpretation of things already seen.

### 6.5 Escape, pursuit and the temple

Mak's understanding of the truth produces flight and a search for protection. The temple and senior monk become crucial to the story's resolution.

**ANALYSIS**
The film shifts from domestic enclosure to institutional refuge:
- the home is the site of attachment and denial;
- the village is the site of partial knowledge and social pressure;
- the temple becomes a place where the haunting can be named and addressed;
- the monk's authority offers a path that neither Mak nor Nak can create alone.

The transition does not make the earlier love false. It reveals that love cannot reverse death.

### 6.6 The resolution: separation, release and religious order

The film's conclusion frames the ending through religious intervention and the possibility of spiritual release. Mak's subsequent religious response is part of the story's moral closure.

**ANALYSIS**
The ending can be read in at least two simultaneous ways:
- as compassionate release from an attachment that cannot be fulfilled in the living world;
- as restoration of social and religious order after a female ghost's desire has become disruptive.

The second reading is not a reason to dismiss the first. It is a tension the film's gender politics invite the viewer to examine.

## 7. Nang Nak: mystery, evidence and psychology

### 7.1 The central mystery

The film's central question is not simply “Is Nak a ghost?” It is:
- What has happened during Mak's absence?
- Why does the domestic world seem normal to Mak but wrong to the village?
- Which signs can be trusted?
- Who knows the truth, and when?
- Can Mak accept a reality that destroys his desired future?
- Is Nak's haunting primarily terror, grief, love, possessiveness, or a mixture?

The audience's experience changes as it moves from domestic intimacy to community suspicion and then to explicit supernatural recognition.

### 7.2 Evidence chain

| Stage | Observable evidence | Mak's interpretation | Community / audience interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separation | War removes Mak from home | Temporary absence | A dangerous gap in family knowledge |
| Return | Nak and child appear at home | Family restored | Audience begins to suspect a temporal impossibility |
| Domestic irregularities | Strange events and reactions around the household | Coincidence / misunderstanding | Possible signs of death and haunting |
| Neighbour warnings | Villagers behave with fear and urgency | Interference or hostility | Community knowledge challenges private belief |
| Revelation | Evidence confirms Nak and child are dead | Denial becomes untenable | Earlier scenes are reclassified |
| Temple intervention | Religious authority addresses the haunting | Protection and painful recognition | A public / spiritual resolution becomes possible |

### 7.3 Mak's psychology: attachment as perceptual defence

Mak's ignorance is not only a puzzle mechanism. It is tied to desire.
- He wants his wife and child to be alive.
- The home appears to offer the life he expected to resume.
- His emotional investment makes contrary evidence difficult to accept.
- The community's warnings threaten not just his belief but his restored identity as husband and father.
- Recognition is therefore a form of bereavement, not simply discovery.

The film turns denial into a dramatic force. Mak does not merely lack information. He has a powerful reason not to integrate it.

### 7.4 Nak's psychology: love, grief and control

Nak should not be reduced to either a harmless romantic ghost or a generic evil spirit.
- Her attachment is sincere within the film's emotional construction.
- Her desire to keep Mak near her is also coercive and dangerous.
- Her death has frozen the relationship in a time that Mak cannot inhabit.
- Her child and household become part of a desired continuity that death has made impossible.
- Her power is not separable from the film's gendered representation of ideal wifehood, sacrifice and devotion.

Fuhrmann's reading of temporal incongruity and Buddhist melancholia helps clarify why the film's horror is emotionally structured around incompatible states of being, rather than a simple living / dead opposition.

### 7.5 The community as a distributed witness system

The villagers do not all know the same things at the same time. The community is a network of:
- direct witnesses;
- rumour carriers;
- fearful neighbours;
- people who avoid the house;
- those willing to confront Mak;
- religious authorities who can interpret and act.

This is important because the truth does not arrive through one omniscient narrator. It emerges socially.

## 8. Nang Nak: visual, spatial and sonic grammar

### 8.1 Domestic space as a continuity trap

The house is visually important because it does not immediately announce itself as a supernatural location. The film builds dread by letting domestic life continue.

**ANALYSIS**
- Familiar architecture creates emotional security.
- Repeated household actions establish a stable baseline.
- The audience gradually notices that this baseline cannot be reconciled with the community's knowledge.
- The house becomes a container for a relationship that time has already ended.
- The supernatural is therefore experienced through the failure of ordinary continuity.

### 8.2 Village geography and the temple

The village provides social visibility. People see, hear and interpret one another. The temple provides a distinct institutional space.

The movement between home, village and temple maps a progression:
1. private attachment;
2. public suspicion;
3. acknowledged supernatural crisis;
4. religious intervention.

This spatial progression is also an information progression.

### 8.3 Period design and heritage aesthetics

Institutional festival notes praise the film's cinematography, period setting, atmosphere and visual effects. Scholarship complicates the claim of historical authenticity.

May Adadol Ingawanij's reading of *Nang Nak* as heritage cinema is especially useful: the film's carefully composed past is also a modern image of the nation, designed for domestic and international spectators. The visual past is not neutral. It is a cultural argument.

### 8.4 Sound and music

Production credits identify Chatchai Pongprapaphan and Pakkawat Vaiyavit in the music credits and Eutthana Tusawut in sound. Contemporary reviews and festival materials note the soundtrack's contribution to atmosphere, but this pass did not establish a cue-by-cue score analysis.

**ANALYSIS**
The soundtrack should be examined in relation to:
- domestic calm and the gradual appearance of anomalies;
- the acoustic texture of village life;
- the contrast between private intimacy and public warning;
- the transition into supernatural confrontation;
- the temple's different acoustic and emotional register.

The film's slow atmosphere depends on sound as part of its world, but exact instrumentation and cue origins should not be asserted without a score or production interview.

## 9. Comparative analysis: Viy and Nang Nak

### 9.1 The central difference

| Dimension | Viy | Nang Nak |
|---|---|---|
| Source chain | Gogol's literary horror story, with contested Ukrainian folklore parallels | Repeated Thai Mae Nak legend, reinterpreted through modern cinema |
| Main dramatic structure | Three-night ritual ordeal | Domestic return, delayed recognition, communal warning and release |
| Protagonist | Young seminarian compelled to perform a vigil | Husband returning from war, emotionally invested in domestic continuity |
| Supernatural figure | Witch / dead woman and named demonic adversary | Dead wife and child |
| Primary boundary | Sacred circle / ritual space and the rule governing the gaze | Domestic attachment and the boundary between living and dead |
| Main pressure | Fear, exhaustion, coercion and ritual failure | Love, denial, grief, community knowledge and religious intervention |
| Social system | Seminary, household authority and ritual obligation | Family, village witness network and Buddhist temple authority |
| Time | Repeated three-night countdown | Delayed recognition after an extended wartime absence |
| Viewer knowledge | Learns the ritual rules and anticipates their failure | Gradually reclassifies apparently ordinary domestic scenes |
| Tone | Comic opening shifting into supernatural spectacle and dread | Romantic tragedy, slow unease, ghost horror and spiritual resolution |

### 9.2 Two kinds of supernatural visibility

*Viy* makes the supernatural increasingly visible. The threat escalates toward an explicit creature encounter. The question is whether the protagonist can withstand what he sees and obey the rule that protects him.

*Nang Nak* initially hides the supernatural inside ordinary domestic life. The question is whether Mak can recognise what the audience and community gradually understand.

The contrast:
- *Viy*: visibility is dangerous.
- *Nang Nak*: failure to recognise is dangerous.

### 9.3 Two different uses of religious authority

In *Viy*, religious procedure is the protagonist's defensive task. The ritual is assigned to him, repeated and tested. Its failure is tied to a breach of the final rule.

In *Nang Nak*, religious authority becomes the agent of interpretation and resolution. Mak cannot negotiate the boundary between love and death alone. The temple offers a social and spiritual process that makes release possible.

This is a functional comparison, not a claim that Christian prayer and Thai Buddhist intervention are equivalent.

### 9.4 The dead woman's agency

Both films risk being flattened if the dead woman is treated only as a monster.
- In *Viy*, the young woman is a supernatural antagonist, but the film's structure also depends on Khoma's vulnerability and the coercive conditions of his vigil.
- In *Nang Nak*, Nak is emotionally central. Her love is legible, but the film also shows the danger of making another person remain inside a relationship they cannot consent to after death.

The second film gives the ghost more emotional and social complexity, but that does not make her actions harmless.

### 9.5 The community's role

*Viy* narrows toward the ritual chamber. Social authority places Khoma inside the ordeal, but the climax is largely a struggle between individual and supernatural force.

*Nang Nak* uses the village as a distributed information system. Community members notice, interpret, warn and respond. The supernatural becomes socially consequential before Mak accepts it.

### 9.6 The role of time

*Viy* compresses dread into a three-night countdown. The audience can count the remaining stages.

*Nang Nak* stretches time across absence, childbirth, death and return. The couple's emotional clocks are misaligned. Mak believes he has returned to the same life, while Nak's life has already ended.

### 9.7 Pair verdict at the narrative level

*Viy* is a story about the failure to maintain a ritual boundary under supernatural pressure. *Nang Nak* is a story about the failure to accept a temporal and ontological boundary because love wants the past to continue.

Both films make horror consequential by placing a human being inside a system that is larger than individual desire.

## 10. CMP-002 comparison

CMP-002's seven-layer model:

**World → Community → Individual → Event → Evidence → Interpretation → Consequence**

Cross-cutting axes:
1. Material Economy
2. Rule and Ritual
3. Time and Memory
4. Power and Voice

### 10.1 Viy through the seven layers

| Layer | Film evidence / analysis |
|---|---|
| World | A stylised rural Ukrainian literary world with seminary, village, household and ritual chamber |
| Community | Religious institution, household authority and local social hierarchy |
| Individual | Khoma's appetite, humour, fear, fatigue and constrained agency |
| Event | Encounter with the witch-like woman, death and three-night vigil |
| Evidence | Bodily experience, ritual disturbances, repeated supernatural attacks and the final apparition |
| Interpretation | Witchcraft, spiritual threat, ritual obligation and the limits of human protection |
| Consequence | Khoma's failure to survive the final ordeal |

**Axis notes**
- **Material Economy:** not the central engine, but household power and social status help compel Khoma into the task.
- **Rule and Ritual:** dominant. Prayer, bounded space, repeated nights and the instruction not to look form the film's procedural architecture.
- **Time and Memory:** the three-night countdown structures anticipation and cumulative exhaustion.
- **Power and Voice:** institutional and household authority constrain Khoma; the supernatural figure increasingly controls the encounter.

### 10.2 Nang Nak through the seven layers

| Layer | Film evidence / analysis |
|---|---|
| World | A reconstructed nineteenth-century Thai village with domestic, social and temple spaces |
| Community | Neighbours, witnesses, family and Buddhist monastic authority |
| Individual | Nak's attachment, Mak's denial, villagers' fear and the monk's spiritual role |
| Event | Mak's return to a household in which Nak and child are dead |
| Evidence | Domestic anomalies, neighbour warnings, deaths and the eventual revelation |
| Interpretation | Love, ghosthood, denial, spiritual disturbance and religious obligation |
| Consequence | Separation, release and the restoration of a religious / social order |

**Axis notes**
- **Material Economy:** war and household survival form the background conditions, though the main conflict is affective and spiritual.
- **Rule and Ritual:** the temple and monk provide the social / spiritual mechanism for addressing the haunting.
- **Time and Memory:** central. The living and dead inhabit incompatible temporal positions.
- **Power and Voice:** Mak's ignorance, Nak's supernatural power, village witness knowledge and monastic authority shape who can define the truth.

### 10.3 What CMP-002 reveals about the pair

The pair offers two different architectures for the relationship between unusual events and existing social systems:
- In *Viy*, the event is contained within an assigned ritual procedure. The protagonist's survival depends on obeying a boundary.
- In *Nang Nak*, the event is embedded in family and community relationships. The protagonist's survival and emotional release depend on accepting knowledge that the community already possesses.

CMP-002's model helps separate the supernatural event from the system that makes it powerful. The ghost or demon is not the whole story. Institutions, relationships, memory and authority determine what the event means and what people can do about it.

## 11. Audience pulse and reception

**Evidence limitation:** The web research for this pass did not produce a robust, directly inspectable Reddit sample for both films. This dossier therefore makes no Reddit consensus claim and includes no invented quotations. The following points are documented critical / institutional reception themes, not quantified audience sentiment.

### Viy: reception themes

- The film is regularly positioned as a rare early Soviet horror feature and as a notable adaptation of Gogol.
- BFI highlights its special effects and nightmarish imagery while also situating it within Soviet genre restrictions.
- The Academy Museum's 2026 programme presents it in a folklore-focused series and explicitly notes the disputed relationship between Gogol's claim of folklore and modern scholarship on Ukrainian parallels.
- Some critical commentary cautions against over-selling it as a modern shock-horror experience. The film can be understood as a supernatural fable with period effects and a strong atmosphere rather than a contemporary jump-scare machine.
- Ptushko's effects and the climactic creature design remain central to its reputation.

### Nang Nak: reception themes

- The International Film Festival Rotterdam describes the film as a major Thai box-office success and praises its atmosphere, visual style and soundtrack.
- The Busan archive highlights its cinematography and special effects.
- Scholarship treats it as a turning point in the modern cinematic representation of Mae Nak.
- Critical debate includes its treatment of gender, idealised wifehood, Buddhist release, heritage aesthetics and the construction of “Old Siam” for modern spectators.
- The film's international success is part of its cultural history, not merely a measure of horror effectiveness.

### Audience-pulse conclusion

A future audience study should sample dated Reddit threads and other audience forums separately for each film, preserve URLs and thread context, distinguish first-time viewers from repeat viewers, and include criticism as well as praise. Until then, no claims about Reddit consensus are made.

## 12. Transferable filmmaking mechanisms

These are craft observations only. They are not story proposals or applications to KARUPPI.

### 12.1 From Viy

1. **Give the supernatural ordeal a visible procedure.** The viewer should understand what the protagonist must do, where, for how long and what rule cannot be broken.
2. **Escalate through controlled repetition.** Repeating the same ritual across multiple nights lets the audience measure how the threat changes.
3. **Use a clear boundary.** A circle, room, threshold or protected zone becomes dramatically useful when the audience understands its function.
4. **Make the protagonist imperfect before the ordeal.** Human appetite, humour and weakness create a richer contrast than a pre-certified hero.
5. **Make authority part of the threat system.** The protagonist may be endangered not only by the supernatural but by the social structure that assigns the task.
6. **Reserve a distinct final rule.** The climax becomes legible when one specific action can undo the protection established earlier.
7. **Let effects serve a dramatic progression.** Creature spectacle should intensify the protagonist's ordeal, not replace it.

### 12.2 From Nang Nak

1. **Hide the supernatural inside ordinary continuity.** Familiar routines can become uncanny when later evidence changes their meaning.
2. **Distribute knowledge across a community.** Different witnesses can know different parts of the truth at different times.
3. **Make denial emotionally motivated.** A character should have a reason to reject evidence beyond simple stupidity.
4. **Let the reveal reclassify earlier scenes.** The strongest clue may be a familiar detail whose meaning changes.
5. **Use social geography as an information map.** Home, village and temple can each represent a different stage of knowledge and authority.
6. **Give the ghost an emotional logic without absolving harm.** Love, grief and coercion can coexist.
7. **Let religious authority have a dramatic function.** It should change what characters can understand or do, rather than appear as decorative cultural texture.
8. **Treat period design as authored interpretation.** A beautiful historical world is still a modern representation of the past.

### 12.3 Pair-level mechanisms

- **Distinguish a rule-based ordeal from an information-based mystery.** One asks whether a procedure can be maintained. The other asks whether a character can accept what the evidence means.
- **Make the community's knowledge legible.** Show who knows, who suspects, who denies and who has authority to act.
- **Use time as a structural pressure.** A three-stage countdown and a delayed return from war create different kinds of suspense.
- **Do not confuse empathy with exoneration.** A character's grief or fear can explain harmful behaviour without making it harmless.
- **Let the supernatural expose the limits of human institutions.** Institutions can protect, compel, interpret or fail.

## 13. Failure modes and cautions

### Viy

- **Calling Gogol's Viy a settled Ukrainian folk entity:** the relationship between the named creature and folkloric parallels is contested.
- **Treating the film as ethnography:** it adapts a literary story and uses stylised Soviet production design.
- **Reducing Khoma to cowardice:** the three-night ordeal is also a story of coercion, exhaustion and unequal power.
- **Treating the ritual circle as generic occult magic:** the film's ritual world is shaped by a specific literary and Christian-coded setting.
- **Overvaluing effects at the expense of structure:** the three-night progression is what gives the effects dramatic meaning.
- **Claiming exact shot or sound timings without a matched print:** current anchors are sequence-level only.

### Nang Nak

- **Treating the 1999 film as the definitive legend:** Mae Nak has a long history of retellings and changing interpretations.
- **Calling the period setting historically exact:** the film is a heritage representation and requires independent verification for historical claims.
- **Reducing Nak to a monster or ideal wife:** the film's gender politics are more complicated.
- **Treating Buddhist authority as a universal exorcism template:** the film's religious world is culturally specific.
- **Reading the community as a single mind:** knowledge and fear are distributed unevenly.
- **Ignoring the modern production context:** the film's nostalgic image of the past is also a late-twentieth-century cultural and industrial construction.
- **Claiming cue-level sound analysis without a score / production source:** production credits are known, detailed cue analysis remains open.

### Comparative

- Do not flatten Christian-coded seminary ritual and Thai Buddhist monastic authority into one generic spiritual system.
- Do not equate a literary monster with a living folk legend.
- Do not treat the dead woman as having the same narrative function in both films.
- Do not assume that a supernatural event becomes socially powerful in the same way in every culture.
- Do not mistake a film's historical setting for a historical document.

## 16. Pair verdict

**Viy makes a ritual boundary visible and then subjects it to three nights of escalating pressure. Nang Nak makes a domestic relationship feel intact until time, death and community knowledge expose the impossibility at its centre.**

Their strongest shared principle is that the supernatural becomes dramatically powerful when it intersects with a human obligation:
- Khoma must perform a vigil he did not freely choose.
- Mak wants to resume a marriage that death has already ended.
- The household in *Viy* can compel a religious service.
- The village in *Nang Nak* can recognise a truth that Mak cannot accept.
- Ritual and religious authority can protect, constrain, interpret or release, depending on the film's particular social world.

The most important difference is where the suspense lives. *Viy* places suspense in a repeated procedure and the danger of breaking a rule. *Nang Nak* places suspense in delayed recognition and the emotional cost of accepting evidence.

**CMP-002 finding:** An unusual event does not act in isolation. Its force depends on the world, community, individual, evidence, interpretation and consequence around it. *Viy* dramatises a ritual system under attack. *Nang Nak* dramatises a family and community system trying to reconcile attachment with death.
