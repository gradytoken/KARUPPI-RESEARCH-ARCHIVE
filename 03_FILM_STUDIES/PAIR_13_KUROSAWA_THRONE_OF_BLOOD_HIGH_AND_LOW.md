# PAIR 13 — Akira Kurosawa: *Throne of Blood* (1957) + *High and Low* (1963)

**Status:** Director-masters comparative dossier completed, source-led  
**Selection:** Two complementary films, one period supernatural tragedy and one contemporary procedural  
**Research mode:** Film research only

## 1. Research question

How does Kurosawa control what the audience can know when the source of danger is either a seemingly prophetic supernatural encounter or a human crime reconstructed through evidence?

The pair deliberately moves across two very different production worlds:

- *Throne of Blood* relocates Shakespeare's *Macbeth* to a stylised feudal-Japanese setting. Prophecy, ambition, guilt, fog, landscape and theatrical performance shape a tragedy whose supernatural presence is part of the film's adaptation of Shakespeare.
- *High and Low* adapts Ed McBain's 1959 crime novel *King's Ransom* into a contemporary Japanese kidnapping thriller. Its first movement concentrates on a wealthy industrialist's moral and financial dilemma. Its later movement becomes a police investigation built from geography, observation, sound and coordinated inference.

**Executive finding:** Both films convert space into a system of pressure. In *Throne of Blood*, the forest and castle world is opaque, ritualised and fate-laden. In *High and Low*, modern space is measurable but socially divided: rooms, hills, streets, telephone positions, sightlines and recorded background sounds become evidence. Kurosawa's range lies partly in his ability to make radically different spaces legible as moral structures.

The comparison is not that supernatural prophecy and criminal investigation are equivalent. One belongs to a Shakespearean tragedy transformed through Japanese cinematic and theatrical forms. The other belongs to a modern police procedural. Their shared craft problem is how knowledge, power and responsibility become visible through cinematic space.

## 3. Source and edition records

### 3.1 *Throne of Blood* (1957)

**SOURCE**
- Original Japanese title: *Kumonosu-jō*.
- Directed by Akira Kurosawa, with screenplay credits to Shinobu Hashimoto, Ryūzō Kikushima, Hideo Oguni and Kurosawa.
- Adapted from William Shakespeare's *Macbeth*, relocated to a feudal-Japanese setting.
- Principal cast includes Toshirō Mifune as Washizu and Isuzu Yamada as Asaji.
- Criterion's edition record lists 109 minutes, black and white, 1.37:1, Japanese-language audio.
- Criterion describes the film as a Macbeth adaptation that combines tragedy with formal elements drawn from Noh theatre.
- Donald Richie's Criterion essay records Kurosawa's long-standing interest in adapting *Macbeth*, his view that the Japanese period film could use modern filmmaking techniques, and his interest in the destructive repetition of power.

**Edition caution**
Use the 109-minute Criterion record as a working reference, not as proof that every release has identical leaders, credits or transfer speed. Verify the actual edition before creating any timestamp table.

### 3.2 *High and Low* (1963)

**SOURCE**
- Original Japanese title: *Tengoku to Jigoku*.
- Directed by Akira Kurosawa.
- Adapted from Ed McBain's 1959 novel *King's Ransom*, written under the name Evan Hunter.
- Principal cast includes Toshirō Mifune as Kingo Gondo and Tatsuya Nakadai as Inspector Tokura.
- Criterion lists 143 minutes, black and white with colour inserts, and 2.39:1.
- BFI's film record lists 142 minutes. BFI's DVD record lists 143 minutes. This is a one-minute catalogue / transfer discrepancy, not evidence of a different narrative cut.
- Criterion describes the film as both a domestic drama and police procedural, with class analysis built into the kidnapping plot.
- BFI's 2025 essay by Jasper Sharp documents the film's two-part structure, the transition from Gondo's domestic crisis to the police investigation, and the role of spatial relations and sound clues.

## 4. Adaptation and cultural boundaries

### 4.1 *Macbeth* is the source structure, not a Japanese historical document

**SOURCE**
Donald Richie's Criterion essay records Kurosawa's own explanation that he saw *Macbeth* as a story whose pattern of power and destructive repetition could speak across historical settings. The adaptation retains the tragic movement of prophecy, ambition, murder and collapse while translating its dramatic world into a stylised Japanese period setting.

Criterion's film note identifies Noh theatre as one of the formal traditions incorporated into the film.

**ANALYSIS**
The supernatural figure in the forest functions within the film's adaptation of Shakespeare's Weird Sisters and prophecy structure. The fog, forest, stillness and formalised performance create a world in which the future appears to have already entered the present. The film's atmosphere is not dependent on explaining a complete supernatural cosmology.

**CULTURAL BOUNDARY**
Do not describe the film's forest spirit as a documentary example of a single Japanese folk belief. The figure is part of a specific Shakespeare-to-film adaptation. Noh is a historically developed and living theatrical tradition, not a generic shorthand for Japanese mysticism. The film's stylisation should be discussed as an artistic synthesis, not as a complete representation of Japanese performance culture.

### 4.2 *High and Low*: literary source, modern Japanese setting

**SOURCE**
The source novel is *King's Ransom* by Ed McBain. BFI notes that Kurosawa used the plot outline but made his own stylistic and thematic approach, turning the material into a work concerned with social divisions and moral relationships. Criterion likewise describes the adaptation as a race-against-time thriller that becomes social commentary on contemporary Japanese society.

**ANALYSIS**
The film does not simply transfer an American detective plot into Japan. The domestic crisis, business pressures, police organisation, city geography and class contrasts are restructured into a Japanese social environment. The setting is not a neutral backdrop: spatial hierarchy gives the title's vertical metaphor a visible form.

**CULTURAL BOUNDARY**
The film is a crafted 1963 representation of contemporary Japan, not a sociological survey of all Japanese business, police or working-class life. Its class argument is a cinematic and dramatic construction. Do not treat the film's criminal, police or slum characters as representative ethnographic evidence.

## 5. Mystery architecture and information control

### 5.1 *Throne of Blood*

**ANALYSIS**
- The forest encounter supplies a prophecy-like interpretive frame before the consequences have fully unfolded.
- Washizu and Asaji process the prediction through desire, calculation and fear. The audience watches an external statement become an internal decision-making pressure.
- The tragedy is cumulative. Each attempt to secure power creates further action and further exposure.
- Knowledge is not absent. The characters receive a warning, but they misread the meaning and limits of what has been said.
- The ending converts the body of the ruler into a public image of political collapse.

**Interpretive distinction**
The film's central uncertainty is not whether every event has a rational explanation. It is how prophecy, ambition, fear and human choice interact in a tragic structure.

### 5.2 *High and Low*

**SOURCE**
BFI's account identifies a major structural shift. The first movement is centred on Gondo's decision and private space. The film then moves into the train sequence and a long police-investigation section in which Gondo recedes and Inspector Tokura's team takes over the narrative.

**ANALYSIS**
- The initial kidnapping call makes the crime a moral dilemma before it becomes a fully mapped police case.
- The mistaken target intensifies the question of responsibility: the ransom demand remains even though the kidnapper has taken the chauffeur's child rather than Gondo's own son.
- The train section turns a private decision into a public race against time.
- The police phase changes the viewer's relationship to evidence. Spatial measurements, maps, public telephones, sightlines, background sounds and repeated observation narrow the possible location.
- The antagonist's delayed visibility keeps the investigation focused on traces and systems before granting a direct confrontation.

**Evidence chain**
BFI's essay notes the importance of mapping which public telephones would have been in direct sunlight and visible from Gondo's house, and the use of a trolley-car sound on a recording as a clue to location. These are not decorative details. The film makes the audience experience the process by which an otherwise ordinary fragment becomes useful evidence.

### 5.3 Pair comparison

*Throne of Blood* begins with a statement about the future and traces how people turn it into action. *High and Low* begins with a criminal act and traces how investigators reconstruct its spatial conditions. The first creates tragic inevitability through misinterpretation and repeated choice. The second creates suspense through controlled accumulation of verifiable clues.

## 6. Character, authority and moral pressure

### *Throne of Blood*
- Washizu's desire for status is inseparable from his fear of losing it.
- Asaji is an active strategist within the marriage and the political crisis, not merely an accessory to Washizu's ambition.
- Authority is shown as a fragile position sustained by violence, obedience and appearances.
- The supernatural encounter does not remove responsibility from the human characters. Their choices remain the mechanism through which the prophecy becomes consequential.

### *High and Low*
- Gondo's business ambition and financial exposure are not separate from the kidnapping decision. The crisis tests what he values when private family loyalty and corporate survival collide.
- The chauffeur's family changes the moral arithmetic. The person whose child is endangered is not the person whose money is demanded.
- Inspector Tokura's team represents coordinated institutional knowledge rather than a lone detective's intuition.
- The kidnapper's social position and grievance are made intelligible as part of the film's social analysis, but intelligibility is not exoneration.

**ANALYSIS**
Both films test the gap between a person's self-image and the consequences of their decisions. One uses a political tragedy. The other uses a modern criminal investigation.

## 7. Visual grammar

### 7.1 *Throne of Blood*
- Fog and forest reduce visual certainty and make direction, distance and destination unstable.
- Castle corridors, gates and open courtyards turn power into a visible arrangement of thresholds and guarded access.
- Strong black-and-white contrast gives faces, armour, mist and landscape graphic weight.
- Formalised movement and stillness support the Noh-related performance influence identified by Criterion.
- The film repeatedly makes the landscape appear larger and more controlling than the individual body.

### 7.2 *High and Low*
- The first section uses the house's elevated position and expansive windows to establish a spatial and social vantage.
- The second section descends into public and urban environments, shifting the visual field from controlled domestic space to a network of routes and observations.
- The widescreen 2.39:1 composition allows people, vehicles, rooms and city distances to be organised within a single frame.
- The selective colour insert, including the film's pink-smoke clue, becomes an information marker within the monochrome world.
- Maps, lines of sight and spatial diagrams are used as active reasoning devices, not merely props.

## 8. Sound grammar

### *Throne of Blood*
**ANALYSIS**
The film's sound world works through atmosphere, controlled musical punctuation, silence and theatrical performance. Noh-derived form should be investigated through the actual soundtrack and performance structure, rather than reduced to a claim that every drum or flute sound is a direct Noh quotation.

**OPEN / NEEDS VERIFICATION**
A cue-by-cue score and sound-effects analysis requires a named edition and verified soundtrack access. This dossier does not assign exact sound cues or claim a full audio transcription.

### *High and Low*
**SOURCE**
BFI identifies the trolley-car sound in the recorded background as a clue used to narrow the location of the kidnapper.

**ANALYSIS**
Ambient sound becomes spatial evidence. A background sound that initially appears incidental can become useful only after the investigators establish a model of where and when the recording was made. This turns listening into a form of mapping.

**TRANSFERABLE MECHANISM**
A sound clue is strongest when it is initially ordinary, later becomes interpretable through context, and changes the next action rather than merely confirming what the audience already knows.

## 9. Sequence anchors

These are chronological navigation anchors, not exact timestamps.

### *Throne of Blood*
1. **Opening landscape and narration:** the ruined castle and mist establish the story as a retrospective tragedy.
2. **Forest encounter:** prophecy enters the characters' decision-making.
3. **Return to the fortress:** political status and ambition become concrete social pressures.
4. **Asaji and Washizu's private deliberation:** interpretation of the prophecy shifts toward action.
5. **The succession crisis:** murder and political violence alter who can safely know what.
6. **The ruler's isolation:** fear begins to overtake the apparent security of power.
7. **Final assault and death:** the political system turns against the ruler it had elevated.

### *High and Low*
1. **Gondo's living room:** private space becomes a decision chamber.
2. **The ransom demand and mistaken child:** the moral problem expands beyond the household.
3. **The financial and family debate:** material cost and moral obligation become inseparable.
4. **The train operation:** space, timing and coordination compress the suspense.
5. **Police handover:** the narrative shifts from a private dilemma to collective investigation.
6. **Mapping and clue reconstruction:** the case is narrowed through physical geography and recorded sound.
7. **Urban pursuit and pink-smoke clue:** visual information becomes actionable.
8. **Confrontation:** the criminal's reasoning is exposed, but explanation does not erase harm.

## 10. CMP-002 model comparison

The archive's seven-layer model is **World → Community → Individual → Event → Evidence → Interpretation → Consequence**, with Material Economy, Rule and Ritual, Time and Memory, Power and Voice as cross-cutting lenses.

| Layer | *Throne of Blood* | *High and Low* |
|---|---|---|
| World | Stylised feudal setting, forest, fortress and political order | Contemporary urban-industrial Japan, home, city and police network |
| Community | Retainers, political hierarchy and military obedience | Family, employees, police, media and class-divided city |
| Individual | Washizu and Asaji interpreting prophecy under ambition and fear | Gondo, the chauffeur's family, Tokura and the kidnapper |
| Event | Prophecy becomes a chain of political violence | Kidnapping becomes moral crisis and police case |
| Evidence | Prediction, testimony, visible consequences and political signals | Calls, maps, telephone locations, sightlines, recording and sound |
| Interpretation | Characters misread the limits of prophecy and power | Investigators test and refine hypotheses |
| Consequence | Collapse of a ruler and political order | Material damage, legal consequence and unresolved class tension |

**Cross-cutting observations**
- **Material Economy:** power and property structure both films, though one uses feudal rank and the other corporate capital.
- **Rule and Ritual:** court hierarchy and theatrical form in one; police procedure and business obligations in the other.
- **Time and Memory:** prophecy and tragic hindsight versus deadline-driven reconstruction.
- **Power and Voice:** who can command, who can be heard, and who controls information.

## 11. Transferable mechanisms

These are abstract craft observations only.

1. **Space can embody hierarchy.** A high vantage, a gated approach or a difficult route can make power visible before a character explains it.
2. **A shift in narrative centre can change genre pressure.** A private dilemma can become a collective procedural without feeling like a new unrelated story when the same event continues to drive the action.
3. **An ordinary sound can become evidence.** The audience must first hear it as part of the world, then understand why it narrows the investigation.
4. **Prophecy is more interesting when it does not cancel choice.** The suspense lies in interpretation and action, not in a mechanical prediction.
5. **Formal contrast can carry thematic contrast.** The visual grammar of a forest tragedy need not resemble the grammar of a modern city procedural to belong to the same director's coherent body of work.

## 12. Failure modes and limits

- Do not treat *Throne of Blood* as a faithful historical account of a particular Japanese period.
- Do not collapse Noh theatre into a generic visual label for slow, strange or supernatural imagery.
- Do not equate Shakespeare's Weird Sisters, the film's forest spirit and Japanese folk belief as interchangeable entities.
- Do not assume *High and Low* represents every aspect of 1960s Japanese policing or class relations.
- Do not treat every clue in *High and Low* as a universal procedural formula. Its clues work because the film establishes specific spatial and temporal conditions.
- Do not romanticise the kidnapper's grievance or turn social explanation into moral absolution.
- Do not infer exact timing from a streaming runtime or a secondary scene description.

## 14. Pair verdict

The pair shows two complementary forms of cinematic knowledge. *Throne of Blood* makes the future feel present through prophecy, landscape and tragic repetition. *High and Low* makes the past recoverable through measured space, recorded sound and collective inference. One moves from ominous foreknowledge toward catastrophe. The other moves from uncertainty toward an evidentiary reconstruction while leaving social and moral questions alive.

Kurosawa's central craft lesson across these two films is not a single camera trick. It is the construction of a world in which space, authority and information determine what characters can do and what the audience can understand.
