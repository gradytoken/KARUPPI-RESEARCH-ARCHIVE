# PAIR 14 — Ingmar Bergman: *The Seventh Seal* (1957) + *Hour of the Wolf* (1968)

**Status:** Director-masters comparative dossier completed, source-led  
**Selection:** One existential allegory and one explicit psychological-folkloric horror film  
**Research mode:** Film research only  
**Canon boundary:** No KARUPPI story explanation, mythology transfer, plot proposal or canon change is authorised.

## 1. Research question

How does Bergman make the invisible visible without giving it one stable meaning?

The pair examines two films in which fear is connected to death, faith, imagination and the limits of human knowledge:

- *The Seventh Seal* places a knight's crisis of faith inside a plague-struck medieval landscape. Death becomes a visible character and a chess opponent, while the film moves between allegory, social observation, religious imagery and intimate human behaviour.
- *Hour of the Wolf* follows an artist living on an isolated island with his wife. Nightmares, diaries, reported memories and disturbing encounters make it difficult to determine where psychological experience ends and the apparently supernatural begins.

**Executive finding:** *The Seventh Seal* externalises existential questions through allegorical figures and a travelling social world. *Hour of the Wolf* internalises fear through fragmented testimony, diary material, unstable perception and an island environment. The first makes mortality visible as a public encounter. The second makes terror intimate, unstable and difficult to verify.

The films should not be treated as two versions of the same supernatural system. One is a philosophical allegory with a broad ensemble. The other is a concentrated psychological horror construction.

## 3. Source and edition records

### 3.1 *The Seventh Seal* (1957)

**SOURCE**
- Original Swedish title: *Det sjunde inseglet*.
- Written and directed by Ingmar Bergman.
- Principal cast includes Max von Sydow as Antonius Block, Gunnar Björnstrand as Jöns, Bibi Andersson as Mia, Nils Poppe as Jof and Bengt Ekerot as Death.
- The Swedish Film Institute / Ingmar Bergman Foundation record lists the 1957 Swedish release at 96 minutes, black and white, 1.37:1, optical mono.
- Criterion lists 97 minutes, black and white, 1.37:1.
- The title is drawn from Revelation 8:1–2. The film's central image of Death playing chess with the knight is connected by Bergman scholarship and institutional material to a medieval church painting he remembered from childhood.
- The Foundation records that shooting began on 2 July 1956 and ended on 24 August 1956. Bergman was given a limited budget and 36 shooting days.
- The opening beach material was filmed at Hovs Hallar. Much of the remainder was made at Råsunda Film Studios.

**Edition caution**
The Foundation's 96-minute Swedish record and Criterion's 97-minute listing differ slightly. Use the actual edition's runtime and credits before timing. The one-minute difference should not be read as evidence of a distinct narrative cut without further documentation.

### 3.2 *Hour of the Wolf* (1968)

**SOURCE**
- Original Swedish title: *Vargtimmen*.
- Written and directed by Ingmar Bergman.
- Principal cast includes Max von Sydow as Johan Borg, Liv Ullmann as Alma Borg, Gertrud Fridh, Georg Rydeberg, Erland Josephson and Ingrid Thulin.
- The Ingmar Bergman Foundation records a Swedish release on 19 February 1968 at 87 minutes. Its technical record lists an original length of 90 minutes.
- Criterion lists 88 minutes, black and white, 1.37:1.
- The Foundation records that the film was shot in 1966, mostly on the coast of Halland at Hovs Hallar, the same coastal location used for the famous beach encounter in *The Seventh Seal*.
- The Foundation notes that material from an earlier abandoned screenplay, *The Cannibals*, was incorporated into *Hour of the Wolf* after Bergman made *Persona*.
- The film uses Alma's account, an interview frame and Johan's diary as part of its narrative presentation.

**Edition caution**
The 87-minute Swedish release, 88-minute Criterion listing and 90-minute technical length are not interchangeable. The actual copy must be identified before timing or comparing the order of brief passages.

## 4. Historical, religious and folkloric boundaries

### 4.1 *The Seventh Seal*: biblical structure and medieval setting

**SOURCE**
The title quotes the Book of Revelation. The Ingmar Bergman Foundation identifies the film as a theological work and notes the biblical reference in the opening. Its production history records the church-painting inspiration and the film's short, constrained production schedule.

The BFI has discussed *The Seventh Seal* within an expansive interpretation of folk horror, especially because of its medieval landscape, superstition, plague imagery and supernatural terror.

**ANALYSIS**
The film combines several registers:
- a biblical-apocalyptic framework,
- a morality-play structure,
- a recognisable medieval social landscape,
- allegorical personification through Death,
- and intimate scenes of food, performance, companionship and ordinary survival.

The allegory does not cancel the humanity of the characters. Its power partly comes from placing enormous metaphysical questions beside modest, practical and compassionate actions.

**CULTURAL BOUNDARY**
The film is not a documentary record of fourteenth-century Swedish religious belief, plague experience or execution practice. Its medieval world is a deliberate dramatic construction shaped by Bergman's memories, biblical imagery, art history and postwar existential concerns. The BFI's folk-horror classification is a critical interpretation, not a universal genre verdict.

### 4.2 *Hour of the Wolf*: film-world demons and folkloric language

**SOURCE**
The Ingmar Bergman Foundation describes the film as the story of an isolated artist plagued by nightmares and visions of demons. It records the film's opening explanation of the “hour of the wolf” as the period between night and dawn when nightmares become powerful and demons are said to be strongest. The Foundation also connects the artist's self-understanding to Bergman's own reflections on creative compulsion.

The BFI argues that *Hour of the Wolf* combines demonic nightmare imagery with folkloric elements and therefore belongs within a broad folk-horror canon.

**ANALYSIS**
The film's supernatural language is deliberately entangled with:
- Johan's artistic self-image,
- insomnia and recurring nightmares,
- the island's isolation,
- Alma's proximity to Johan's experiences,
- diary fragments,
- and the uncertain status of the people Johan encounters.

The horror grows because the film does not provide one stable authority capable of verifying each experience.

**CULTURAL BOUNDARY**
The phrase “hour of the wolf” and the demon rules presented by the film should not be repeated as a verified universal Swedish or ancient European folk belief. They are film-world language unless independently corroborated by a specific folklore source. The aristocratic visitors and demonic figures are not an ethnographic record of Swedish rural communities. The BFI's genre classification is an interpretive framework, not evidence that every depicted belief is traditional folklore.

## 5. Mystery architecture and uncertainty

### 5.1 *The Seventh Seal*

**ANALYSIS**
- The opening encounter gives Death a visible body and a clear conversational relationship with the knight.
- The chess game provides a simple recurring structure through which time, delay and mortality can be measured.
- The travelling narrative introduces different responses to the same social conditions: cynicism, faith, fear, humour, exploitation and compassion.
- The knight seeks certainty about God but receives silence. The film does not replace that silence with a simple supernatural explanation.
- The ending creates an image that is visually decisive but metaphysically open.

**Mystery type**
The central question is not a conventional whodunit. It is an existential inquiry: what can a person know, and what remains meaningful when certainty is unavailable?

### 5.2 *Hour of the Wolf*

**ANALYSIS**
- The film establishes an interview frame around Alma's account of Johan's disappearance.
- Diary material provides a second channel of information, but it does not automatically become objective truth.
- Johan's encounters with the island's inhabitants destabilise the boundary between person, performance, memory and nightmare.
- Alma is both a witness to Johan's deterioration and a narrator whose own experience is implicated.
- The title's night-time interval becomes a temporal threshold in which ordinary verification is weakened.
- The film withholds a clean diagnostic or supernatural solution.

**Mystery type**
The viewer must evaluate the reliability and limits of several forms of evidence: spoken recollection, written diary, visual experience and another person's interpretation.

### 5.3 Pair comparison

*The Seventh Seal* gives uncertainty a public, allegorical form. Death can be seen and addressed, but the meaning of existence remains unresolved. *Hour of the Wolf* makes uncertainty private and perceptual. The viewer cannot confidently separate an external supernatural encounter from the destructive effects of fear, memory and insomnia.

## 6. Character, authority and moral pressure

### *The Seventh Seal*
- Antonius Block is not simply afraid of dying. He is troubled by the possibility that life and the universe have no answer to his demand for meaning.
- Jöns offers a contrasting mode of survival: scepticism, blunt speech and practical observation.
- Jof and Mia offer an alternative to grand philosophical abstraction through family, performance and ordinary tenderness.
- Death is not a conventional villain with a personal grievance. The character is an embodiment of an unavoidable condition.

### *Hour of the Wolf*
- Johan's artistic identity is bound to pain, compulsion and self-alienation.
- Alma becomes narrator, witness and emotional participant. Her role cannot be reduced to an objective camera for Johan's mind.
- The island's social visitors appear to possess their own forms of power, but their status and reality remain uncertain within the film's structure.
- Intimacy does not guarantee shared knowledge. Living beside another person can bring a witness close to an experience without making that experience fully knowable.

**ANALYSIS**
Both films place a central relationship under pressure from an invisible or unknowable condition. One distributes this pressure across a community. The other concentrates it within a marriage and a fragile narrative record.

## 7. Visual grammar

### 7.1 *The Seventh Seal*
- High-contrast black-and-white cinematography gives the beach, sky and figures a stark graphic character.
- The chessboard creates a compact geometric arena for an abstract confrontation.
- Open landscapes make the characters appear exposed to forces larger than themselves.
- Group compositions and the final silhouette turn individual mortality into a collective image.
- Studio interiors and location exteriors create a controlled alternation between theatrical space and open world.

### 7.2 *Hour of the Wolf*
- The isolated coast and island setting create a geography with limited escape routes and little social support.
- Black-and-white imagery reduces visual distraction while allowing faces, shadows, rock and water to carry psychological weight.
- Close framing and shifts in visual continuity help make perception unstable.
- Rooms and gathering spaces can feel theatrical, as though the visitors are performing roles for Johan or for the viewer.
- Diary and interview framing gives the visual narrative more than one level of mediation.

**Cross-film observation**
Both films use Hovs Hallar, but for different dramatic purposes. The location functions in *The Seventh Seal* as an exposed threshold for a direct encounter with Death. In *Hour of the Wolf*, the island coast belongs to a private psychological and social trap.

## 8. Sound grammar

### *The Seventh Seal*
**SOURCE**
The Foundation's production record lists music and songs associated with the film, including Erik Nordgren compositions and the use of the medieval hymn *Dies irae*.

**ANALYSIS**
Music and vocal performance operate within a world where religious imagery, travelling performers and the threat of plague coexist. Sound can mark collective ritual and human fellowship, not only terror.

### *Hour of the Wolf*
**SOURCE**
The Foundation's film record lists the use of Bach's *Partita no. 3 in A minor* and Mozart's *The Magic Flute*.

**ANALYSIS**
The film's sound design should be studied as a relationship between sparse musical references, speech, silence and the credibility of narrated experience. The viewer must keep listening to distinguish what is said by a witness from what is experienced by Johan.

**OPEN / NEEDS VERIFICATION**
A cue-by-cue sound analysis requires a named edition and verified soundtrack access. This dossier does not claim a complete transcription or exact cue positions.

## 9. Sequence anchors

These are chronological navigation anchors, not exact timestamps.

### *The Seventh Seal*
1. **Beach encounter:** the knight meets Death and initiates the chess game.
2. **Return from the Crusades:** the social environment reveals the consequences of plague, fear and religious authority.
3. **Travelling encounters:** the knight and squire meet people whose responses to suffering differ sharply.
4. **Religious spectacle and accusation:** collective fear turns into public ritual and judgement.
5. **The actors' family:** ordinary tenderness interrupts the film's metaphysical argument.
6. **The continuing chess game:** the recurring match links separate episodes into one temporal structure.
7. **Final group image:** the film converts mortality into a stylised collective silhouette.

### *Hour of the Wolf*
1. **Alma's interview frame:** the film establishes testimony and disappearance as its narrative entry point.
2. **Island life and diary material:** the couple's isolation and Johan's internal state become visible.
3. **The hour-of-the-wolf explanation:** the film defines its own nocturnal threshold.
4. **The visitors and the castle gathering:** social performance and apparent demonic presence destabilise Johan.
5. **The artist's account and flashback material:** the viewer must assess the relation between recollection, diary and visual presentation.
6. **The encounter with the past and with Veronica:** intimacy, shame and fear become entangled.
7. **The disappearance and retrospective frame:** the film withholds a simple explanation of what the viewer has witnessed.

## 10. CMP-002 model comparison

The archive's seven-layer model is **World → Community → Individual → Event → Evidence → Interpretation → Consequence**, with Material Economy, Rule and Ritual, Time and Memory, Power and Voice as cross-cutting lenses.

| Layer | *The Seventh Seal* | *Hour of the Wolf* |
|---|---|---|
| World | Plague-struck medieval landscape and religious order | Isolated island, household and nearby estate |
| Community | Travellers, performers, clergy, soldiers and frightened townspeople | Small circle of spouses, visitors and interviewers |
| Individual | Knight, squire, performers and Death as allegorical figure | Johan and Alma, with the artist's self-image under pressure |
| Event | Return from the Crusades and the chess game with Death | Deterioration, encounters and disappearance |
| Evidence | Visible Death, public rituals, testimony and social observation | Diary, interview, memory, visual experience and witness account |
| Interpretation | Faith, scepticism, meaning and mortality | Nightmare, psychological crisis, demonic encounter and unreliable memory |
| Consequence | The limits of certainty and the value of immediate human care | Loss, fractured intimacy and unresolved disappearance |

**Cross-cutting observations**
- **Material Economy:** the first film's survival conditions are public and collective; the second's material world is narrowed to the island household and estate.
- **Rule and Ritual:** biblical and ecclesiastical imagery in one; the film's own invented nocturnal language and social rituals in the other.
- **Time and Memory:** the chess game structures time in *The Seventh Seal*; diaries and retrospective narration complicate time in *Hour of the Wolf*.
- **Power and Voice:** public religious authority and the voice of Death contrast with the unequal reliability of Johan's account and Alma's testimony.

## 11. Transferable mechanisms

These are abstract craft observations only.

1. **Give an abstract problem a recurring physical structure.** The chess game makes mortality and delay dramatically measurable.
2. **Let a stable motif change meaning as the story progresses.** A repeated encounter can accrue emotional weight without requiring repeated exposition.
3. **Use competing evidence channels.** An interview, diary and remembered experience can make the viewer work without making every source equally reliable.
4. **Make geography influence perception.** Isolation is not just a setting fact. It reduces the number of witnesses, alternatives and routes through which characters can test their beliefs.
5. **Do not confuse visibility with certainty.** A visible figure can remain philosophically mysterious, and an unseen cause can remain psychologically powerful.
6. **Preserve ordinary human behaviour inside an allegorical world.** Meals, performance, care and humour keep the film from becoming a lecture.

## 12. Failure modes and limits

- Do not use *The Seventh Seal* as a historical source for actual fourteenth-century Swedish beliefs or plague conditions.
- Do not treat the film's Death character as a universal representation of all European or Christian traditions.
- Do not describe the film's folk-horror status as settled taxonomy. Attribute it to critical sources such as the BFI and note that genre definitions vary.
- Do not assume the film's “hour of the wolf” phrase is a universal or ancient folk expression without independent folklore evidence.
- Do not treat Johan's experiences as a clinical case study or diagnose him from cinematic behaviour.
- Do not assume Alma's narration is an objective correction of Johan's perception. The frame itself is part of the uncertainty.
- Do not equate demonic imagery with a specific Swedish community's actual beliefs.
- Do not infer exact timestamps from the runtime or a third-party plot summary.

## 14. Pair verdict

*The Seventh Seal* turns uncertainty into a public philosophical encounter. Its chess game provides a form through which mortality can be confronted, even though the largest questions remain unanswered. *Hour of the Wolf* turns uncertainty into a private crisis of perception. Its testimony and diary structure prevent the viewer from settling on one final account of the frightening events.

Together, the films show Bergman's ability to move between allegorical clarity and psychological instability. The visible supernatural figure in one film does not solve the mystery of existence. The apparently supernatural world in the other does not erase the psychological and relational dimensions of fear.
