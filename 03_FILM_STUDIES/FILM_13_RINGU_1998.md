**Status:** Reserve-shelf standalone study completed, source-led

**Research mode:** Film analysis only  
**Canon boundary:** No KARUPPI story explanation, mythology transfer, plot proposal or canon change is authorised.

## 1. Research question

How does *Ringu* turn a domestic media object into a carrier of supernatural threat, and how does its investigation connect folklore, recorded images, family history and the material limits of analogue technology?

Hideo Nakata's *Ringu* adapts Koji Suzuki's 1991 novel. A cursed videotape is linked to a sequence of deaths. Journalist Reiko Asakawa investigates the phenomenon after a relative dies, and the investigation becomes personal when her son encounters the tape.

The film's horror does not depend only on the figure of Sadako. It develops through a chain of mediated evidence: testimony, a copied recording, fragmented images, a well, family history, and the television set. The curse is both supernatural and reproducible.

**Executive finding:** *Ringu* makes media transmission part of the threat's mechanics. The VHS tape is a physical object with a visible body, a copyable signal and a place in ordinary domestic life. The film's central formal innovation is the collapse of the boundary between the recorded image and the viewer's space. The final television sequence is not simply a monster reveal. It makes the screen itself an unreliable border.

The film also differs materially from Suzuki's novel. Nakata changes the protagonist from a male journalist to a woman, makes Sadako's visual form more explicit, and reshapes the tape's imagery. The novel's viral and biological dimensions are not transferred intact into the film. Claims about the film must not be borrowed from the novel without marking the difference.

## 3. Source and edition record

**SOURCE**
- Original Japanese title: *リング* (*Ringu*).
- Directed by Hideo Nakata. Screenplay by Hiroshi Takahashi, based on Koji Suzuki's 1991 novel *Ring*.
- Released in Japan in 1998.
- Principal cast: Nanako Matsushima as Reiko Asakawa, Hiroyuki Sanada as Ryūji Takayama, Rikiya Ōtaka as Yōichi, Miki Nakatani as Mai Takano, and Yūko Takeuchi as Tomoko.
- Cinematography by Jun'ichirō Hayashi; music by Kenji Kawai; production design by Iwao Saitō; editing by Nobuyuki Takahashi.
- Runtime commonly listed at approximately 96 minutes. Catalogue and transfer variations exist, so the actual edition should be recorded before timing.
- Produced during the late-1990s expansion of Japanese horror cinema and became a major international reference point for J-horror.
- The film's VHS image, television screen and domestic viewing environment are central to its horror design.

**Edition caution**
The 1998 Japanese theatrical film must be distinguished from the 1995 television adaptation *Ring: Kanzenban*, the 1999 sequel *Ringu 2*, the 2002 American remake *The Ring*, and later franchise entries. This dossier concerns Nakata's 1998 film only.

## 4. Source-text and cultural boundaries

### 4.1 Koji Suzuki's novel versus Nakata's film

**SOURCE**
Suzuki's novel *Ring* was published in 1991. The novel's protagonist is male journalist Kazuyuki Asakawa. The film changes the protagonist to Reiko Asakawa, a female journalist and mother. The film also reshapes the cursed tape's imagery and Sadako's presentation.

Scholarship on the novel-film relationship identifies a major difference in how contagion operates. The novel's viral theme is more explicit and connected to biological and scientific material. The film gives the videotape a different visual and historical emphasis, with the curse experienced through grainy images, fragmented scenes and the crossing of the television boundary.

Valerie Wee's *Cinema Journal* article compares the video images in *Ringu* and *The Ring*, showing how the tape's visual construction participates in cultural and aesthetic differences between Japanese and Hollywood horror.

Olga V. Solovieva's chapter, "Horror Old and New: Nakata Hideo's *Ringu* (1998) between J-Horror and Hibakusha Cinema," argues that the film's gray-toned, damaged video imagery can be read in relation to Japanese historical anxieties and the visual memory of nuclear trauma. This is a scholarly interpretation, not a statement that the film is literally about Hiroshima or Nagasaki.

**ANALYSIS**
The tape is not a neutral plot device. It is an image-object whose form is part of the threat. The viewer sees a sequence that appears to contain evidence, but its fragments are not immediately legible. The investigators must interpret the images, and the film audience performs the same work.

**CULTURAL BOUNDARY**
Do not transfer the novel's full virus / biological explanation into the film. Do not treat the film's Sadako as a documentary representation of a real historical person or as a definitive portrait of all Japanese ghosts. The film draws on and transforms traditions; it does not exhaust them.

### 4.2 Yūrei, onryō and theatrical traditions

**SOURCE**
Marisa Mourinha's 2020 article on *Ringu* and *The Ring* analyses how the ghostly figure relates to Japanese tradition, including folklore and theatrical forms such as kabuki and bunraku. The article argues that the film's ghost is shaped by a cultural history of supernatural representation, while also creating a new form tied to video media.

The film's visual Sadako is recognisable through the white clothing, long black hair obscuring the face, unusual movement and the bodily crossing from the screen. These visual choices resonate with established yūrei / onryō iconographies, but the exact combination is Nakata's cinematic construction.

**CULTURAL BOUNDARY**
Yūrei and onryō are not interchangeable labels for every Japanese ghost. The categories have varied histories and artistic representations. Do not treat white clothing, long hair or a particular movement as universal fixed folklore rules. The film's imagery is a modern cinematic adaptation, not a complete ethnography of Japanese supernatural belief.

### 4.3 VHS technology and the historical moment

**SOURCE**
The BFI's 2018 feature describes the VHS tape's physical qualities: its bulky casing, visible magnetic tape, fragility and tactile presence. The article situates *Ringu* in a moment when videotape was still a familiar domestic technology but was already beginning to be displaced by DVD.

The film makes the domestic television a site of danger. The videotape enters an ordinary household, is inserted into an ordinary machine, and appears on a screen that viewers normally regard as a safe frame separating representation from reality.

**ANALYSIS**
The technology is frightening because it is ordinary and intimate. The tape can be handled, copied, stored and replayed. Its reproducibility allows the curse to move through a recognisable social network.

**CULTURAL BOUNDARY**
The film's curse mechanics are fictional. Real VHS recordings do not transmit supernatural effects. The material properties of analogue video can support the film's atmosphere, but they are not evidence of paranormal transmission.

## 5. Sequence progression

Sequence anchors, not timecodes.

### R1. The opening deaths and the rumour

The film introduces the rumour of a videotape associated with death. The opening establishes a pattern of teenage testimony and unexplained mortality.

**ANALYSIS**
The curse begins as social information. Before the audience sees the tape, it hears about it. Rumour is the first transmission channel.

### R2. Reiko investigates

Reiko, a journalist, follows the story after the death of her niece Tomoko and her friends. She approaches the event as an investigation, collecting testimony and looking for a material explanation.

**ANALYSIS**
Her professional role makes the supernatural claim something that can be researched. The film's evidence chain begins with ordinary journalistic procedure.

### R3. The cabin and the tape

Reiko locates the place where the young people watched the tape and discovers the recording. She watches it.

**ANALYSIS**
The tape is presented as a sequence of images without an immediately accessible narrative. Its fragments appear to be meaningful but do not yet form a stable explanation.

### R4. The deadline

Reiko becomes aware that the tape is associated with a one-week deadline. The threat becomes temporal and personal.

**ANALYSIS**
The film turns the curse into a countdown without reducing the mystery to a conventional ticking-clock device. The audience knows there is a deadline but does not yet understand the mechanism.

### R5. Ryūji joins the investigation

Reiko asks Ryūji for help. His unusual perceptual abilities and scepticism create a second interpretive perspective. Together they examine the tape and search for the source of its images.

**ANALYSIS**
The investigation becomes a collaboration between different forms of knowledge. Reiko brings journalistic persistence and personal urgency. Ryūji brings a more detached, analytical and sometimes morally troubling perspective.

### R6. The tape's fragments become clues

The images are revisited and interpreted. The investigators identify geographic, linguistic and historical clues. The film gradually connects the recording to a family and a place.

**ANALYSIS**
The tape is both an object to decode and a witness to an event. It contains traces that are not organised for the viewer's convenience. Interpretation is an active process.

### R7. The well and the past

The investigation leads toward the well and the history of Sadako. The film reconstructs the circumstances of her death and the connection between the past event and the present curse.

**ANALYSIS**
The well is a physical container of history. The investigation's movement from tape to place reverses the normal direction of media: a recorded image sends the characters back toward the material event that produced it.

### R8. The attempt at appeasement

Reiko and Ryūji attempt to address the past through the recovery and burial of Sadako's remains.

**ANALYSIS**
The film briefly invokes a traditional expectation that identifying the dead and restoring proper treatment may settle the disturbance. The later events show that this expectation is insufficient.

### R9. Ryūji's death

The curse reaches Ryūji. The film stages the supernatural event through the television screen, with Sadako's body crossing from the recorded image into the room.

**ANALYSIS**
The screen boundary collapses. The audience has watched the tape within the film and has watched the film itself on a screen. The scene makes those two viewing positions resonate.

### R10. The copy and the final transmission

Reiko realises that copying the tape may be connected to survival. The film ends with the curse's reproduction becoming part of the protagonist's next decision.

**ANALYSIS**
The ending transforms the question from "how do I stop the curse?" into "what does survival require me to transmit?" The curse's logic becomes ethical and social, not merely supernatural.

## 6. Mystery architecture and evidence chain

| Dimension | *Ringu* |
|---|---|
| Primary disturbance | A videotape associated with death one week after viewing |
| Focal position | Reiko's journalistic and maternal investigation |
| Main uncertainty | What is the tape, who created it, and how does the curse operate? |
| Evidence form | Testimony, VHS images, geographic clues, family history, well and remains |
| Media role | The tape is both evidence and carrier |
| Space | Domestic television, rental / viewing spaces, rural locations and the well |
| Time | One-week deadline, recorded past and present investigation |
| Reveal structure | Fragment decoding, historical reconstruction, apparent appeasement, failed resolution |
| Ending function | The curse's transmission becomes an ethical choice and unresolved social process |

### Evidence chain

1. A rumour describes a videotape and a death pattern.
2. Reiko collects testimony after Tomoko's death.
3. The cabin provides access to the recording.
4. The tape's fragments appear to contain clues.
5. Ryūji helps interpret the images.
6. The investigation connects the tape to Sadako's history.
7. The well provides a material location for the past event.
8. Recovery and burial appear to offer appeasement.
9. Ryūji's death demonstrates that the attempted solution was incomplete.
10. Copying the tape becomes a possible means of survival and a mechanism of further transmission.

**Key distinction:** The tape does not simply reveal a mystery. It participates in the mystery's propagation.

## 7. Character psychology and authority

**Reiko Asakawa**
- A journalist who approaches the deaths through investigation.
- Her role shifts from professional inquiry to maternal urgency.
- Her choices become ethically difficult when survival depends on copying the tape.
- The film's gender shift from the novel's male protagonist changes the emotional and relational structure.

**Ryūji Takayama**
- Reiko's former husband and investigative collaborator.
- His analytical abilities and psychic sensitivity complicate ordinary evidentiary rules.
- His relationship with Reiko combines intellectual cooperation, distance and shared responsibility.
- His fate demonstrates the limits of their attempted explanation.

**Yōichi**
- Reiko's son.
- His exposure to the tape changes the stakes from a personal investigation to an urgent family threat.
- His presence intensifies the film's maternal dimension.

**Sadako**
- The film gradually reconstructs her life and death.
- Her ghost is frightening, but the investigation gives the audience a history of victimisation and betrayal.
- The film's visual presentation withholds her face, making the body and movement more important than facial expression.

**Authority dynamics**
The film moves among journalistic investigation, scientific / psychological interpretation, family knowledge, local testimony and supernatural evidence. No single institution has full control of the information.

## 8. Visual grammar

- **VHS image texture:** Grain, noise, unstable movement and low-resolution imagery make the tape feel materially different from the film's main image.
- **Fragmented montage:** The tape's images are not presented as a conventional explanatory flashback. Their order and meaning must be reconstructed.
- **Screen within screen:** The television becomes a second frame inside the film. The viewer watches characters watching an image.
- **Well geometry:** The well creates a vertical axis linking the surface world to the buried past.
- **Gray palette:** Nakata's visual treatment gives the film a muted and drained appearance. Solovieva's scholarly interpretation links this to historical memory and nuclear imagery, a reading that must remain attributed.
- **Sadako's face:** Her hair conceals the face, withholding ordinary facial recognition and making movement, silhouette and bodily presence central.
- **Domestic setting:** The television and video player are ordinary household objects, making the supernatural intrusion intimate rather than distant.
- **Boundary violation:** The final television sequence turns the screen from a protective frame into a passage.

**SOURCE**
The BFI's 2018 article analyses VHS as a physical and cultural object. Valerie Wee's *Cinema Journal* article studies the cursed video as a visual construction. The Arrow Video booklet reproduces material from Donato Totaro's 2000 interview with Nakata, including the director's childhood association between the well and a television monitor as a tube or connection to hell.

## 9. Sound grammar

- Kenji Kawai's score uses a restrained, atmospheric sound world rather than constant musical escalation.
- The tape's degraded sound and image quality make it feel like a found object rather than a polished film sequence.
- The recording's voice fragments and ambient noises function as clues.
- Silence and room tone are important around the television and the well.
- The final sequence combines the familiar domestic sound environment with an impossible visual event.
- Sound helps distinguish the tape's recorded world from the present-day investigative world.

**OPEN / NEEDS VERIFICATION**
A full production interview with the sound team and a detailed mix breakdown were not verified in this research pass. Specific recording chains, microphones or post-production methods should not be invented.

## 10. Space, time and memory

| Axis | *Ringu* |
|---|---|
| Space | Television room, cabin, local sites, family spaces and well |
| Time | One-week countdown; recorded past; delayed return of the dead |
| Memory | Tape fragments preserve a traumatic event without explaining it |
| Evidence | Image fragments, testimony, location, family history and remains |
| Reproduction | Copying the tape extends the curse's reach |
| Closure | The immediate investigation produces partial knowledge but the curse remains transmissible |

**ANALYSIS**
The film's most important spatial relation is not simply inside versus outside. It is recorded image versus viewing room. The television screen is a boundary that seems safe until the final breach. The well and the screen become connected as vertical passages through which the past reaches the present.

### Four cross-cutting axes

**A. Material Economy**
- VHS rental, recording, copying and household playback.
- The tape is a commodity and a carrier, not an abstract digital file.
- The technology's ordinary domestic presence is essential to the threat.

**B. Rule and Ritual**
- Watch the tape, receive a deadline, investigate the images, attempt to interrupt the sequence.
- The rule system is gradually learned by the characters and audience.
- Copying changes the apparent survival condition and produces an ethical consequence.

**C. Time and Memory**
- The one-week interval structures suspense.
- The tape carries images from the past into the present.
- The past is not safely archived; it is active and reproducible.

**D. Power and Voice**
- Reiko's journalism gives her access to testimony but not control over the curse.
- Sadako's history is reconstructed through fragments and the accounts of others.
- The tape gives the dead a form of transmission, but not necessarily a coherent verbal testimony.

## 12. Folklore, media and historical interpretations

**SOURCE**
Mourinha's 2020 study situates Sadako's ghost in relation to Japanese folklore and theatrical forms, including kabuki and bunraku. Wee's article examines how the tape's imagery differs between the Japanese original and the American remake. Solovieva's chapter argues for a reading of the film through the visual memory of nuclear trauma and Japanese postwar history.

Carlos Rojas's work on viral contagion in the *Ringu* intertext analyses how the novel and its adaptations make media replication part of the story's structure. The viral metaphor is especially explicit in Suzuki's novel and should not be automatically projected onto every detail of Nakata's film.

**ANALYSIS**
The film supports several levels of interpretation:
1. A ghost story about a murdered woman whose past remains active.
2. A media horror film in which a recording becomes a carrier of danger.
3. A story about the ethics of reproduction and survival.
4. A film that can be read in relation to Japanese historical anxieties and postwar visual memory.

These readings can coexist, but they are not interchangeable. The fourth is a scholarly interpretation, not an established one-line allegorical key.

**CULTURAL BOUNDARY**
Do not claim that the film is a direct allegory of Hiroshima or nuclear trauma as a settled fact. Attribute this reading to scholarship. Do not flatten yūrei, onryō, kabuki and bunraku into a single generic "Japanese ghost tradition." Do not treat VHS technology as supernatural in reality.

## 14. Production findings that materially affect interpretation

1. Nakata adapted Koji Suzuki's 1991 novel, but changed the protagonist from Kazuyuki to Reiko and reshaped the tape's imagery.
2. The VHS format is central to the film's tactile and domestic horror.
3. The film's final screen-crossing sequence turns the boundary between recorded image and present space into the climax.
4. The Arrow Video booklet reproduces Nakata's explanation of a childhood memory of a well and his association of the television monitor with a tube or passage.
5. Kenji Kawai composed the score.
6. The film's visual Sadako draws on recognisable ghost iconography but is a specific cinematic design.
7. The novel's viral and biological dimensions differ from the film's emphasis on visual trauma, ghostly return and media reproduction.

## 15. Failure modes and analytical cautions

- Treating *Ringu* and the 2002 American *The Ring* as the same film.
- Treating the 1995 television adaptation as Nakata's 1998 feature.
- Importing the novel's full biological / viral explanation into the film.
- Calling all Japanese ghosts yūrei or onryō without distinction.
- Treating the cursed videotape as an allegory with only one correct meaning.
- Assuming the tape's images are a neutral, complete record of Sadako's life.
- Treating the final screen breach as merely a jump scare rather than a violation of the viewing frame.
- Ignoring the gender change from the novel's male journalist to the film's mother-journalist.
- Assuming that burial or ritual appeasement is a universal Japanese supernatural rule.
- Treating the film as evidence about real VHS technology or paranormal transmission.

## 16. Transferable craft mechanisms

These are abstract observations about cinematic construction, not story proposals.

1. **Make the medium part of the threat's form.** A recording can be both clue and carrier.
2. **Give the evidence an incomplete grammar.** The audience should be able to notice details before it understands their relation.
3. **Make reproduction consequential.** Copying, sharing or replaying can alter the story's social reach.
4. **Use a frame as a boundary.** A screen, window or recording frame can feel protective until the film violates it.
5. **Connect a present object to a buried event.** A contemporary device can carry an older history without erasing its material specificity.
6. **Distinguish source-text mechanics from adaptation mechanics.** A film can preserve a premise while changing the underlying explanation.
7. **Let investigation become ethical.** Discovering the mechanism may force a protagonist to decide what to transmit.
8. **Use sound and image as separate evidence streams.** The audience can receive clues from one channel before the other.
9. **Give a recurring image a changing function.** An image may begin as an unexplained fragment and later become testimony.
10. **Preserve cultural specificity.** The ghost's visual design should be understood through its own history, not as a universal monster template.

## 19. Standalone study verdict

*Ringu* is a key reserve title because it expands the research programme beyond the haunted building and the visible ghost. Its central horror is a reproducible media object that turns viewing into participation.

The film's formal achievement lies in making the VHS tape both an archive and an active carrier. Its images are fragments of a past event, but they also create a future threat. The screen, normally a boundary between representation and the viewer's space, becomes permeable. The well and television are connected not because they are literally the same object, but because both become passages through which the past reaches the present.

The adaptation from Suzuki's novel matters. Nakata changes the protagonist, the visual grammar and the relationship between media, memory and contagion. The novel and film must therefore be compared carefully rather than collapsed into one source.

The film's most important craft distinction is **the curse as a reproducible image system**. The threat is not only what the audience sees. It is what the act of viewing makes possible next.
