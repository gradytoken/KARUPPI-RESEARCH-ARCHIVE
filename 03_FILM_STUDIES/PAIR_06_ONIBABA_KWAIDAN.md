## 0. Research question and executive finding

### Research question

**How can horror emerge from two radically different relations between the human world and the supernatural: one grounded in bodily survival and a hostile landscape, the other staged as a sequence of exquisitely composed encounters with the dead?**

The films share a year, a national cinema and an interest in the porous boundary between ordinary life and the uncanny. Their methods are sharply different.

- *Onibaba* strips its world down to hunger, war, sex, fear, labour and a landscape that seems to swallow people. Its supernatural charge gathers around a demon mask whose use begins as deliberate deception but becomes psychologically and physically consequential.
- *Kwaidan* builds four literary ghost stories into a deliberately artificial world. Painted skies, controlled colour, formal movement, stylised sets, silence and Takemitsu's experimental sound make the supernatural less an intrusion into realism than the governing aesthetic of the film.

**Pair finding:** *Onibaba* makes the human body and the landscape feel trapped in a survival system. *Kwaidan* makes the world feel like a threshold between the living and the dead, rendered through theatrical artifice. One works by stripping away ornament until material need becomes monstrous. The other works by making artifice so complete that beauty itself becomes uncanny.

The pairing is useful because it prevents a narrow definition of folklore horror. A supernatural story does not need to look documentary-realistic, and a film can be terrifying without confirming a supernatural event. Conversely, a highly stylised ghost story can remain emotionally legible when its visual world is far from naturalism.

## 2. Cultural and literary source boundaries

### 2.1 Onibaba: the Buddhist fable and Shindo's transformation

**SOURCE**
A 2023 peer-reviewed article by Teng-Kuan Ng, “After Great Pain: The Uses of Religious Folklore in Kenji Mizoguchi’s *Sansho the Bailiff* and Kaneto Shindo’s *Onibaba*,” identifies Shindo's source as “The Mask of Flesh,” a fable derived from the medieval Buddhist collection *Ofumi*, associated with Rennyo (1415–1499). The account describes an older mother-in-law who wears a demon mask to frighten her widowed daughter-in-law away from frequent temple visits. In the fable, the mask adheres to her face and is removed only through contrition and invocation of Amida Buddha.

That is not the plot of *Onibaba*. Shindo relocates and transforms the mask motif into a wartime survival drama. The film's older woman uses a mask as a weapon in a conflict involving desire, jealousy and control. The mask's meaning is no longer simply a didactic warning about piety. It becomes a device through which the film examines the instability of human identity, the violence of possession and the return of fear to the person who deploys it.

**ANALYSIS**
The transformation matters more than a superficial claim that the film “uses Japanese folklore.” Shindo takes a recognisable moral fable structure and changes its social and emotional engine:
1. The source fable's central issue is religious conduct and the moral danger of obstructing another person's piety.
2. The film places the older woman and younger woman inside a material economy shaped by war, scarcity and the absence of men.
3. The mask becomes a tool of interpersonal coercion, not merely a supernatural punishment.
4. The film intensifies the mask's physical horror by making it an object that appears to alter the face and the social identity of its wearer.
5. The film's final moral and emotional field is not reducible to the source fable's explicit religious lesson.

The adaptation is therefore neither a faithful retelling nor an ethnographic record. It is a modern cinematic reworking of a Buddhist narrative resource.

**CULTURAL BOUNDARY**
Do not say that the film documents a universal Japanese belief that a demon mask sticks to a person's face. The mask's adhesive supernatural function belongs to the particular fable and Shindo's cinematic transformation. Do not infer a historical practice from a prop. The word “Onibaba” is commonly glossed as “demon woman” or “demon hag,” but translation alone does not establish one fixed folkloric entity or a single canonical legend.

### 2.2 Kwaidan: Hearn as collector, translator and literary mediator

**SOURCE**
The film adapts four stories associated with Lafcadio Hearn, also known by his Japanese name Koizumi Yakumo. The source trail crosses more than one Hearn collection:
- “The Black Hair” derives from “The Reconciliation,” in *Shadowings* (1900).
- “The Woman of the Snow” and “Hoichi the Earless” are associated with *Kwaidan: Stories and Studies of Strange Things* (1904).
- “In a Cup of Tea” comes from *Kotto: Being Japanese Curios, with Sundry Cobwebs* (1902).

The title *Kwaidan* is a romanisation of kaidan, a term for ghost / supernatural tales. The Academy, Criterion, Japan Society and other institutional records identify Hearn as the source writer and Yoko Mizuki as screenwriter.

**CULTURAL BOUNDARY**
Hearn's books are literary mediations, not transparent field recordings of an untouched folk culture. His versions were shaped by selection, translation, narration, literary style and the publishing context in which he wrote. A scholarly discussion of the film notes that Hearn's literary retellings included European literary flourishes and were also presented to readers through the imagined appeal of “ghostly Japan.” That history does not invalidate the stories. It does mean that the film's source chain is layered:

**oral / written Japanese story traditions → Hearn's collection, translation and literary retelling → Yoko Mizuki's screenplay → Kobayashi's cinematic staging.**

The four episodes should not be flattened into one generic “Japanese ghost belief.” Their narrative sources, motifs and local contexts differ. In particular, Yuki-onna is a recognisable folkloric figure, but the film's exact characterisation is one artistic version. Hoichi's story draws on the biwa-hoshi / Heike narrative tradition, but the film's staging and sound are authored decisions. The cup story's unfinished quality is part of Hearn's literary text and becomes a deliberately strange closing gesture in the film.

### 2.3 Comparative source integrity

The films use cultural material in different ways:
- *Onibaba* transforms a specific Buddhist moral fable into a modern, erotic and materially grounded drama.
- *Kwaidan* adapts literary retellings of multiple ghost narratives into a four-part visual and sonic anthology.

Neither film should be used as a documentary source for “what Japanese people believe.” The proper object of study is how filmmakers adapt, select, transform and stage inherited material.

## 3. Onibaba: narrative architecture and scene progression

### 3.1 The opening: a hole as an organising image

**SOURCE**
The subtitle record opens with the image-language of a hole, darkness and a passage from ancient time into the present. The opening frames the landscape as more than a location. A deep pit is introduced as a central piece of the film's material geography.

**ANALYSIS**
The pit performs several jobs at once:
- It is a practical disposal site for murdered samurai.
- It is an economic mechanism. The women convert dead bodies into objects they can sell for food.
- It is a visual counterweight to the open grass field. The film's world has a vast horizontal surface and a vertical opening that swallows bodies.
- It makes death an everyday labour process rather than a distant event.
- It becomes a physical image of concealment: evidence disappears downward, but the act of concealment does not remove its moral or psychological consequences.

The hole is not just a spooky location. It is infrastructure for the household's survival economy.

### 3.2 The killing-and-selling cycle

The women ambush displaced or lost samurai, kill them, strip their armour and weapons, and sell the goods for grain. The war remains largely outside the frame, but its consequences arrive in bodies, weapons, hunger and unstable social order.

**ANALYSIS**
This is an unusually efficient way to make war present without staging a conventional battle:
1. The war produces armed men who become vulnerable when separated from their units.
2. Their displacement brings them into the women's territory.
3. The women turn the violence of the larger conflict into a small-scale survival economy.
4. The goods pass from battlefield to pit to market.
5. The household's apparent stability depends on the continuation of violence elsewhere.

The women are neither romanticised as innocent victims nor reduced to stock monsters. They commit murder, but the film gives their actions a harsh material context. Survival explains the behaviour without absolving it.

### 3.3 Hachi's return and the household's unstable equilibrium

Hachi returns from war and brings information about the son's death. He also brings a new desire into the household. The younger woman is drawn toward him. The older woman fears losing her companion, labour partner and emotional anchor.

**ANALYSIS**
The triangle is a resource conflict as much as a sexual one:
- The older woman needs the younger woman's labour and presence.
- The younger woman wants a life not defined by the older woman's control.
- Hachi offers sexual access and the possibility of escape from the existing arrangement, but he is not a stable route to security.
- The older woman's jealousy is entangled with fear of abandonment and economic vulnerability.

The film's eroticism is not an ornamental subplot. It destabilises the survival unit. Desire threatens to redistribute labour, loyalty and access to food.

### 3.4 The grass field as active geography

The susuki grass field repeatedly obscures bodies, divides sightlines and makes movement difficult to read. It is visually open but informationally restrictive.

**ANALYSIS**
The field creates a paradox:
- The characters are exposed to the sky and wind.
- Their bodies disappear inside the vegetation.
- A person may be close but not visible.
- Sound can travel while visual confirmation remains incomplete.
- Repeated routes and repeated movement turn the field into a maze without walls.

The environment does not need to behave supernaturally to produce dread. It makes ordinary human uncertainty physically plausible.

### 3.5 The demon mask as an instrument of control

The older woman acquires a mask from a samurai. She uses it to frighten the younger woman and disrupt her movement toward Hachi. The mask's frightening appearance is initially a tool: she chooses when to wear it, how to appear and whom to frighten.

**ANALYSIS**
This is a critical transition in the film's mystery design. The mask begins as a controlled performance, then threatens to escape the performer's control.
- The wearer manipulates another person's perception.
- The victim cannot immediately distinguish a human intention from a supernatural threat.
- The mask changes the social meaning of the wearer's face.
- The costume / prop becomes a temporary identity.
- The fear returns to the person who tried to manufacture it.

The mask is not merely “a monster face.” It is a technology of intimidation that becomes a trap.

### 3.6 The mask's removal and the body beneath it

The later sequence makes the mask's relationship to the older woman's face a bodily problem. The terror is not only that she looks monstrous. It is that the boundary between disguise and body seems to have failed.

**ANALYSIS**
The fable's logic is transformed into visceral cinema. The older woman has used the mask to control the younger woman's behaviour. Now she must confront the physical consequences of the identity she performed. The scene's force depends on the viewer remembering that the mask was first chosen deliberately.

The sequence also creates a moral complication. The older woman's suffering is real, but it does not erase her coercion. The film refuses an easy moral reset in which punishment makes the character innocent.

### 3.7 The ending: the leap and the unresolved human threat

The ending returns the film to bodily movement, pursuit and the dangerous geography of the pit and grass. It does not offer a tidy restoration of the earlier household.

**ANALYSIS**
The final movement resists a single neat interpretation:
- It can be read as a desperate assertion of human agency.
- It can also be read as a continuation of the same violence that has governed the film.
- The older woman may be trying to cross the distance between herself and the younger woman, but the film does not convert that movement into uncomplicated reconciliation.
- The body remains the site where fear, desire, labour and punishment collide.

**OPEN / NEEDS VERIFICATION**
A precise shot-by-shot account of the ending requires direct comparison against the exact restored edition being used. This dossier records the structural function of the ending, not a frame-accurate breakdown.

## 4. Onibaba: mystery, evidence and psychology

### 4.1 What is actually mysterious?

The film does not primarily ask the audience to solve a supernatural puzzle. Much of its central conflict is visible and human:
- Who controls the household?
- Who has access to Hachi?
- Who is using fear to control whom?
- Can the older woman maintain her position?
- What happens when the mask's performance becomes physically consequential?

The supernatural uncertainty clusters around the mask's effect. The broader story is driven by legible human motives.

### 4.2 Evidence chain

| Stage | Observable evidence | Character interpretation | Dramatic effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Survival routine | Bodies, armour, weapons and grain exchange | Violence is necessary to live | Establishes the material system |
| Hachi returns | His account of the son's death and his proximity to the younger woman | The old arrangement is threatened | Creates relational instability |
| Mask appears | A frightening face and a concealed wearer | The wearer can manipulate another's fear | Turns appearance into a weapon |
| Mask becomes difficult to remove | The face / mask boundary appears physically compromised | The disguise may have acquired power over its wearer | Converts performance into bodily horror |
| Final confrontation | Pursuit, movement and unresolved physical danger | Control cannot be restored by fear alone | Refuses easy closure |

### 4.3 Psychology without diagnosis

**ANALYSIS**
The older woman's behaviour can be understood through a cluster of pressures:
- fear of losing her only remaining household partner;
- dependence on the younger woman's labour;
- sexual jealousy and possessiveness;
- fear of ageing and diminished power;
- an environment in which ordinary social protections have collapsed.

These are interpretive descriptions, not clinical diagnoses. The younger woman is also not simply an object of desire. Her wish for sexual and personal freedom exists inside a dangerous economy in which men, war and scarcity constrain her choices.

### 4.4 Moral design

The film makes a strong distinction between explanation and exoneration. Material deprivation explains why the women kill. It does not erase the dead men or make the violence morally neutral. The older woman's fear of abandonment is understandable. Her use of the mask to control another person remains coercive.

This tension is a major source of the film's adult unease. The audience is not given a morally clean position from which to judge.

## 5. Onibaba: visual, spatial and sonic grammar

### 5.1 Visual grammar

**SOURCE**
Criterion describes the film's black-and-white widescreen imagery, windswept marshes, dark eroticism and striking lyrical / macabre images. The production record credits Shindo with production design and Kiyomi Kuroda with cinematography.

**ANALYSIS**
- **Horizontal repetition:** the grass field becomes a visual field of recurring movement. The characters seem to run through a world that never changes, even as their relationships change.
- **Occlusion:** vegetation conceals bodies and makes the frame an incomplete source of information.
- **Vertical opposition:** the hole cuts downward through the otherwise horizontal landscape. It is the place where the household hides the consequences of its actions.
- **Body against environment:** bodies are frequently reduced to moving shapes within grass, mud, darkness and wind.
- **Face as unstable identity:** the mask changes the meaning of the face, then threatens the wearer's sense of bodily continuity.
- **Widescreen pressure:** the broad frame gives the field scale, but does not guarantee safety or visibility.

### 5.2 Sound grammar

**SOURCE**
The score is by Hikaru Hayashi. Criterion characterises it as frenzied. The film's sound record is credited to Tetsuya Ohashi.

**ANALYSIS**
The sound world is not a soft atmospheric blanket. Percussive and rhythmic energy can make movement feel compulsive. Wind and the rustle of the grass bind the characters to the environment. Breathing, exertion and bodily proximity reinforce the film's physical register.

Sound can suggest a presence without identifying it. The viewer may hear movement or a body before obtaining a clear visual account. In this setting, the soundscape belongs to both the landscape and the people moving through it.

**OPEN / NEEDS VERIFICATION**
A full cue-by-cue musical analysis would require a matched score / soundtrack transcription and direct listening against a named restoration. This dossier does not invent cue titles, instrument assignments or exact cue timecodes.

### 5.3 Editing and rhythm

The repeated ambush-and-sale pattern establishes a routine. Hachi's return changes the meaning of the routine without changing the basic geography. The mask episode then breaks the older woman's assumption that she can control the rhythm of fear.

The film's repetitive actions are not redundant. Repetition establishes the household's survival loop, so that a small deviation in behaviour carries large consequences.

## 6. Kwaidan: anthology architecture and segment analysis

### 6.1 The anthology as a designed sequence

The four stories are not simply four interchangeable ghost incidents. They vary in scale, emotional emphasis and narrative closure.

| Segment | Central human breach | Supernatural pressure | Narrative movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Black Hair | A samurai abandons his first wife for status and wealth | Return, memory and the uncanny body of the abandoned wife | Regret becomes horror |
| The Woman of the Snow | A man breaks a vow of silence | A snow spirit's promise and threat | A private encounter returns years later |
| Hoichi the Earless | A performer becomes bound to the dead through repeated performance | A phantom court summons the biwa player | Ritual protection fails through omission |
| In a Cup of Tea | A man encounters an unexplained face in a cup and drinks | An unfinished apparition enters the story's frame | The film closes on deliberate incompletion |

### 6.2 The Black Hair

**SOURCE**
The segment is adapted from Hearn's “The Reconciliation,” in *Shadowings*. A poor samurai leaves his loving first wife to marry into a wealthier household and advance his status. He later returns, filled with regret.

**ANALYSIS**
The story's horror is built through a delayed recognition. The returning husband expects emotional continuity: he imagines that the past can be recovered by returning to the same place. The film uses repetition and a measured camera movement to make the return feel like an attempted repair of time.

The domestic room becomes a trap because it appears to preserve what the character abandoned. The horror does not require a complicated monster mythology. It requires the emotional impossibility of restoring a relationship after betrayal.

**Craft observation**
The same visual approach can first promise reunion and then expose decay. A repeated camera movement changes meaning because the audience's knowledge has changed.

### 6.3 The Woman of the Snow

**SOURCE**
The segment adapts Hearn's Yuki-onna story. A woodcutter witnesses a supernatural killing in a snowstorm. The woman spares him on condition that he never speak of the encounter. Years later, he tells the story to his wife, who is revealed to be the being from the snow.

**ANALYSIS**
The supernatural threat is organised around a promise. The initial encounter creates a private rule. Years of apparently ordinary domestic life make that rule feel distant, perhaps safely forgotten. The act of narration breaks the boundary between past encounter and present household.

The film makes the uncanny return through intimacy rather than a public spectacle. The wife is not simply an external monster arriving at the door. She is already inside the man's life.

**Cultural boundary**
Yuki-onna is a widely recognised figure in Japanese folklore, but regional variants and literary versions differ. Hearn's version and Kobayashi's film should not be mistaken for the single authoritative form of the tradition.

### 6.4 Hoichi the Earless

**SOURCE**
The segment draws on Hearn's “The Story of Mimi-nashi Hoichi.” Hoichi is a blind biwa player who performs the story of the Heike for a phantom audience. A Buddhist temple attempts to protect him by inscribing sacred text across his body, but the ears are omitted. The spirits take the unprotected ears.

**ANALYSIS**
This is the anthology's most elaborate social and ritual system:
- Hoichi's performance attracts the dead.
- The phantom audience has a hierarchy and expects a specific form of service.
- The temple recognises the danger and attempts a protective intervention.
- Protection depends on a procedure being completed.
- A single omission becomes fatal.

The episode is not simply “ghosts attack a musician.” It is about performance, memory, ritual protection, the authority of religious institutions and the vulnerability created by an incomplete procedure.

Hoichi's blindness is narratively significant. He cannot verify the audience through sight. His musical skill is the channel through which the dead establish contact with him.

### 6.5 In a Cup of Tea

**SOURCE**
The final segment adapts a story from Hearn's *Kotto*. A samurai sees a face in a cup of tea and drinks. The figure later appears in a more direct form. The source story is unfinished, and the film preserves an abrupt, open-ended quality.

**ANALYSIS**
The final segment shifts the anthology's scale. The previous stories have recognisable narrative arcs. This one foregrounds the instability of storytelling itself. The narrator's framing becomes part of the experience. The audience is left with a story that appears to have been interrupted or escaped its container.

The ending is not a failed resolution. It changes the rules of closure. The film's final movement suggests that an uncanny encounter may not be contained by the narrative that introduces it.

### 6.6 Anthology-level pattern

The first three stories are strongly shaped by broken promises, obligations or protective boundaries. The final story destabilises the act of telling. Across the anthology, the supernatural arrives through a human relation that has been breached, mismanaged or incompletely understood.

The film does not insist that every ghost has the same origin or motive. Its unity comes from formal design, recurring themes and a coherent aesthetic world.

## 7. Kwaidan: mystery, evidence and psychology

### 7.1 The rules are local to each story

Unlike a single-protagonist mystery, *Kwaidan* does not establish one universal supernatural rulebook. Each segment has its own local logic:
- betrayal and return in “The Black Hair”;
- vow and disclosure in “The Woman of the Snow”;
- performance and ritual protection in “Hoichi the Earless”;
- reflection, ingestion and narrative incompletion in “In a Cup of Tea.”

The viewer learns the rules of each episode through consequence rather than a master exposition scene.

### 7.2 Evidence chains by segment

| Segment | First sign | Human response | Confirmation / complication |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Black Hair | A return to the apparently preserved domestic past | The samurai accepts the scene as reconciliation | The reunion becomes a bodily and temporal horror |
| The Woman of the Snow | A supernatural encounter in the storm | A vow of silence is made | Years later, disclosure reveals that the past remains active |
| Hoichi the Earless | A summons from an unseen audience | Hoichi performs repeatedly | The temple's incomplete protection exposes the cost of omission |
| In a Cup of Tea | A face appears in liquid | The samurai drinks | The apparition returns and the story refuses conventional closure |

### 7.3 The viewer's knowledge

The film's stylisation does not mean that the viewer is always uncertain about whether a ghost exists. In many segments the supernatural is accepted within the story's world. The mystery lies elsewhere:
- What obligation has been created?
- What does the spirit want?
- What human act has activated the consequence?
- Can the character recognise the rule before it is too late?
- Can a ritual or promise contain what has crossed into the human world?

This is different from an ambiguity-led film. The supernatural often has narrative reality, while the human characters struggle to understand its terms.

### 7.4 Psychological design

The emotional engine changes from episode to episode:
- **The Black Hair:** regret, status anxiety, abandonment and the fantasy that time can be reversed.
- **The Woman of the Snow:** fear, secrecy, intimacy and the danger of breaking a promise.
- **Hoichi:** artistic absorption, vulnerability, institutional protection and the cost of a procedural omission.
- **In a Cup of Tea:** curiosity, narrative play and the inability to control what one has invited into the story.

The anthology gains depth by refusing one repeated emotional explanation for all four encounters.

## 8. Kwaidan: visual and sonic grammar

### 8.1 The artificial world

**SOURCE**
The Academy describes the film's use of Eastmancolor Tohoscope, elaborate sets and electronically inflected music. Queensland Art Gallery / Gallery of Modern Art notes that the film was shot on constructed sound stages, using vast hand-painted sets and surreal landscapes. Its programme notes identify links to Japanese theatre, classical storytelling and scroll painting.

**ANALYSIS**
The film does not conceal its constructed nature. Its world is painted, composed and staged. This is not an attempt to imitate documentary reality. It creates a visual system in which colour, shape and space can carry emotional and supernatural information.

The backgrounds can feel less like locations than states of mind. A painted sky is not a failed natural sky. It is an authored plane that makes the world appear deliberately unreal.

### 8.2 Colour as narrative structure

Colour in *Kwaidan* is not merely decorative richness. It separates emotional zones and gives each segment a distinct visual identity. The artificial environment lets colour become a structural element, not just a property of photographed objects.

The effect is especially important because the film does not depend on darkness in the conventional horror sense. Its images can be bright, expansive and beautiful while remaining threatening.

### 8.3 Framing and theatricality

The formal compositions create a sense that characters are being placed within an already existing design. Human movement can appear choreographed against a painted environment. This gives the supernatural a ritualised quality.

The frame often feels like a stage or picture plane. That does not flatten the drama. It makes the characters' vulnerability more visible because their movements seem constrained by a world whose rules have already been set.

### 8.4 Sound: Takemitsu's designed uncanny

**SOURCE**
Toru Takemitsu is credited for music and sound design. The Los Angeles Public Library's discussion of his film work describes the use of the biwa, traditional instruments and electronic alteration. TCM's account includes Takemitsu's explanation of using actual wood and deliberately produced cracking / tearing sounds for “The Black Hair.”

**ANALYSIS**
The sound design is not simply an accompaniment to the image. It constructs an alternate acoustic reality:
- everyday materials can produce sounds that feel unfamiliar;
- silence can become an active event;
- a sudden sound can interrupt the composed visual field;
- traditional instruments can be transformed by recording and electronic treatment;
- the biwa in “Hoichi” is both a musical instrument and a narrative channel between the living and the dead.

The score and effects are not separable into a simple opposition between “music” and “sound.” The film's acoustic design makes the ordinary world feel authored by an unseen intelligence.

### 8.5 Silence and duration

Silence in *Kwaidan* is not an absence of design. It can make the audience attend to the visual surface, anticipate a sound, or experience the scale of a space. The film can stretch a moment until the viewer becomes aware of waiting itself.

### 8.6 Segment-specific sonic functions

- **The Black Hair:** sound can make domestic materiality uncanny. Wood and room sounds become expressive rather than neutral.
- **The Woman of the Snow:** the snowstorm and the sparseness of the sound world reinforce exposure, cold and the fragility of the vow.
- **Hoichi the Earless:** the biwa and recitation connect performance to memory, history and the phantom audience. The sound is not merely heard by the dead; it summons and sustains their world.
- **In a Cup of Tea:** the final segment's framing and sound help preserve its unfinished, uncanny quality.

## 9. Comparative analysis: the films side by side

### 9.1 The central difference

| Dimension | Onibaba | Kwaidan |
|---|---|---|
| Basic horror engine | Human survival, desire and coercion under wartime scarcity | Supernatural encounters shaped by vows, betrayal, performance and narrative boundaries |
| World | Material, exposed, repetitive and physically dangerous | Artificial, composed, theatrical and aesthetically self-contained |
| Landscape / space | Grass field, pit, marsh and routes of concealment | Painted sets, staged rooms, snow landscapes and ritual spaces |
| Body | Labouring, hungry, sexual, vulnerable and masked | Often composed as an image within a supernatural tableau |
| Mask / face | A disguise that becomes a bodily trap | Spectral faces and appearances emerge through story-specific rules |
| Evidence | Human behaviour is legible; the mask's effect is uncanny | Supernatural presence is often accepted; the rules and consequences are the puzzle |
| Sound | Rhythmic, percussive, bodily and environmental | Experimental, composed, often sparse, with transformed material sounds |
| Time | Repetition of survival routines, disrupted by desire | Compressed story-time, delayed consequence and ritual recurrence |
| Moral pressure | Survival explains violence but does not erase responsibility | Broken vows, betrayal and incomplete protection produce consequences |
| Formal strategy | Stripping down | Elaborate stylisation |

### 9.2 Shared structure: a boundary is crossed

Both films make the uncanny consequential when a human boundary is violated:
- In *Onibaba*, the mask crosses the boundary between performance and bodily identity. Desire crosses the boundary of the household's fragile arrangement.
- In *Kwaidan*, a vow is broken, a marriage is betrayed, a performance binds the living to the dead, or an unexplained apparition enters an everyday object.

The films differ in what the boundary protects. *Onibaba* is concerned with control over bodies and resources. *Kwaidan* is concerned with obligations, memory and the limits between worlds.

### 9.3 Different relations between the viewer and the supernatural

*Onibaba* keeps much of its horror close to human behaviour. Its supernatural element is concentrated in the mask. The viewer is asked to read motive, deception and consequence.

*Kwaidan* more often establishes the supernatural as a fact within the episode. The viewer is asked to learn the terms of an encounter and watch a character fail to respect, understand or escape them.

The distinction is useful:
- **Ambiguity about cause** is not the same as **uncertainty about rules**.
- **A human deception that becomes uncanny** is not the same as **a supernatural presence with a local code of conduct**.

### 9.4 Beauty and ugliness

*Onibaba* makes beauty dangerous through the erotic and visual power of the grass field. The landscape can be lyrical and still be an instrument of concealment.

*Kwaidan* makes beauty dangerous through control. Its painted worlds are visibly artificial, but the precision of the design makes them feel governed by rules that human characters cannot master.

In both films, beauty does not provide safety. It can intensify the sense of entrapment.

## 10. CMP-002 comparison

CMP-002's seven-layer model is:

**World → Community → Individual → Event → Evidence → Interpretation → Consequence**

Its four cross-cutting axes are:
1. Material Economy
2. Rule and Ritual
3. Time and Memory
4. Power and Voice

### 10.1 Onibaba through the seven layers

| Layer | Film evidence / analysis |
|---|---|
| World | A war-disrupted rural landscape of grass, marsh, pit, hunger and armed displacement |
| Community | A fractured social order in which ordinary protection and authority are weak or absent |
| Individual | Two women and Hachi act from desire, fear, jealousy and the need to survive |
| Event | Murders, Hachi's return, the younger woman's affair and the older woman's mask performance |
| Evidence | Bodies, armour, market exchange, concealment, the mask and its apparent effect |
| Interpretation | Survival necessity, jealousy, deception and supernatural punishment compete as explanatory frames |
| Consequence | The household's fragile arrangement collapses into bodily danger and unresolved conflict |

**Axis notes**
- **Material Economy:** unusually explicit. Violence is converted into food through a repeatable chain.
- **Rule and Ritual:** the Buddhist fable provides a source pattern, but Shindo transforms its moral structure. The household itself has informal rules of labour and loyalty.
- **Time and Memory:** the dead son's absence and the return of a war companion alter the present. Repeated routines make change visible.
- **Power and Voice:** the older woman attempts to control the younger woman's movement through fear. The mask is a power instrument.

### 10.2 Kwaidan through the seven layers

| Layer | Film evidence / analysis |
|---|---|
| World | A deliberately artificial world where supernatural encounters are part of the story's governing reality |
| Community | Households, courts, temples, audiences and narrators create local systems of obligation |
| Individual | Characters act from regret, fear, artistic absorption, curiosity and desire |
| Event | A return, a broken vow, a ghostly summons or an apparition in a cup |
| Evidence | Repeated domestic images, a supernatural encounter, a performance, an omitted protective inscription |
| Interpretation | Each episode teaches a local supernatural rule through consequence |
| Consequence | Betrayal returns, secrecy fails, ritual protection proves incomplete, or the narrative remains unresolved |

**Axis notes**
- **Material Economy:** prominent in the samurai's status-seeking in “The Black Hair,” less central in the other segments.
- **Rule and Ritual:** central. Vows, temple protection, performance obligations and the boundaries between living and dead govern the episodes.
- **Time and Memory:** decisive. The past returns through a domestic space, a broken promise, historical performance and an unfinished narrative.
- **Power and Voice:** who can command performance, who can speak, who must remain silent and who controls the story are recurring concerns.

### 10.3 What CMP-002 reveals about the pair

The films show two distinct ways that a strange event becomes socially powerful:
- *Onibaba* embeds the uncanny inside an already violent material system. The supernatural intensifies a conflict that exists before the mask appears.
- *Kwaidan* builds local social and ritual systems around supernatural encounters. The event becomes legible through the rules that characters have accepted, violated or failed to complete.

This is not a universal formula for folklore horror. It is a comparative observation from these two films.

## 12. Transferable filmmaking mechanisms

These are craft observations only. They are not story suggestions or applications to KARUPPI.

### 12.1 From Onibaba

1. **Make geography perform a narrative function.** The grass field hides bodies and interrupts sightlines; the pit disposes of evidence and materialises the cost of survival.
2. **Let a routine establish the baseline.** Repeated killing and selling make a small relational change feel consequential.
3. **Make the environment both beautiful and dangerous.** The same field can be lyrical, erotic, disorienting and threatening.
4. **Use a prop as a social instrument before it becomes uncanny.** The mask first has a clear human purpose. Its later horror is stronger because the viewer knows how it was used.
5. **Keep motivation morally complicated.** Scarcity explains action without making the action harmless.
6. **Connect erotic desire to material consequences.** Desire changes the distribution of labour, loyalty and security.
7. **Let sound arrive before visual certainty.** Environmental sound can make a concealed space feel populated without supplying proof.

### 12.2 From Kwaidan

1. **Give each anthology segment a distinct local rule.** A viewer can learn the supernatural logic through consequence rather than a global exposition system.
2. **Use artificiality deliberately.** A constructed world can create stronger dread than an imitation of naturalism.
3. **Make colour structural.** Colour can organise emotional and supernatural space.
4. **Treat sound as world-building.** Material noises, silence and altered instruments can make an ordinary space feel governed by another reality.
5. **Build horror around obligations.** A vow, a performance duty or a protective ritual can create a clear dramatic rule.
6. **Let the past remain active.** A long interval does not necessarily neutralise an earlier encounter.
7. **Use omission as a precise failure mechanism.** Hoichi's unprotected ears make a small procedural gap dramatically decisive.
8. **Allow a story to end by refusing containment.** An unfinished narrative can be an intentional formal effect when the preceding structure prepares the audience for it.

### 12.3 Pair-level mechanisms

- **Differentiate ambiguity from rule-learning.** Decide whether the audience is uncertain about what happened or understands the event but not its rules.
- **Make boundaries concrete.** A mask, a vow, a performance, a household arrangement or a threshold gives the uncanny something precise to cross.
- **Let formal style carry information.** Visual and sonic design should alter what the viewer knows, expects or fears, not merely decorate the scene.
- **Preserve consequence.** Once a boundary is crossed, the social or bodily world should not reset without cost.

## 13. Failure modes and cautions

### Onibaba

- **Over-symbolising the film:** The pit and mask invite metaphor, but the film also insists on hunger, labour, sex and violence as material realities.
- **Romanticising survival:** Poverty and war explain the women's actions but do not erase the victims.
- **Reducing the older woman to a jealous villain:** Her actions are coercive, but her fear is rooted in a fragile survival arrangement.
- **Treating the mask as generic Japanese demon lore:** The specific Buddhist fable and Shindo's transformation must remain visible.
- **Treating the field as an active supernatural entity:** Its concealment and acoustic effects are cinematic geography; they do not prove the landscape is literally sentient.
- **Claiming exact shot timing from subtitle anchors:** Subtitle timestamps locate text, not every cut or visual beat.

### Kwaidan

- **Flattening four stories into one mythology:** Each episode has its own source and local rules.
- **Calling Hearn's versions unmediated folklore:** Translation and literary retelling are part of the source history.
- **Mistaking stylisation for cultural authenticity:** Painted sets and theatrical forms are deliberate artistic choices, not a neutral representation of Japanese life.
- **Treating all spirits as morally identical:** The episodes differ in motive, relation and consequence.
- **Ignoring cut history:** Runtime differences affect pacing and the experience of the anthology.
- **Overexplaining the final episode:** Its incompletion is part of its design.
- **Claiming exact cue or shot timings without a matched restoration:** The available evidence supports formal analysis, not a fabricated technical breakdown.

### Comparative

- Do not force the two films into a single “Japanese folklore horror” category.
- Do not assume that shared national origin implies shared cultural logic.
- Do not confuse the supernatural's narrative status with the film's degree of ambiguity.
- Do not treat either film as a direct ethnographic record.

## 16. Pair verdict

**Onibaba turns survival into a closed circuit of hunger, violence, desire and concealment. Kwaidan turns inherited ghost stories into a designed world where beauty, sound and broken obligations expose the limits of human control.**

Their strongest shared principle is not “Japanese folklore.” It is the dramatic force of a boundary that people believe they can manage:
- the older woman believes she can use the mask without being changed by it;
- the samurai believes he can return to the past without consequence;
- the woodcutter believes a vow can be safely buried by time;
- Hoichi's protectors believe a ritual is complete when one crucial part has been omitted;
- the samurai in the final tale believes he can consume an apparition without inviting it into his world.

The films' formal strategies are opposite but complementary. *Onibaba* makes the world frightening by reducing it to elemental material pressures. *Kwaidan* makes the world frightening by transforming it into an elaborate visual and acoustic construction.

**CMP-002 finding:** A strange event gains force when it enters a system of material needs, social obligations, remembered history and unequal power. In *Onibaba*, the system is a survival economy. In *Kwaidan*, it is a set of local moral, ritual and narrative boundaries. The event matters because the world around it already has rules, and because characters misunderstand the cost of crossing them.
