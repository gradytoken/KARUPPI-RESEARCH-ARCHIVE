# FILM-001: Rosemary's Baby — Selected Sensory Grammar Notes

**Study scope:** Selected observations from prior horror-grammar research.  
**Status:** Not a complete film study or timestamped scene breakdown.

## 1. Ordinary sound becomes impossible through context

The wall-sound example works because characters discuss hearing neighbours through a wall, and chanting / flute sounds are heard in that context. Later, the baby's cry is heard from a place that appears empty while the neighbours are away in Europe.

The craft mechanism is not simply "use a creepy sound." A familiar sound becomes disturbing because the surrounding facts make its source or presence impossible.

## 2. Lullaby as a shifting motif

The lullaby is associated with Rosemary humming / singing. The music uses variations of the tune, and Rosemary sings it to the baby.

The useful mechanism is repetition with changed emotional context. The melody can retain recognition while its meaning changes.

## 3. Transferable principle

A sensory motif is strongest when:
- It is introduced in an ordinary context.
- The audience learns its expected source or meaning.
- A later scene changes the context.
- The sound or image now implies something that the characters cannot comfortably explain.

## 4. KARUPPI application

Potential use only, not canon:
- Establish ordinary village sound before the 3 PM darkness or torch procession changes its context.
- Give recurring sounds a consistent source before allowing a later occurrence to contradict what the audience knows.
- Do not make every sound supernatural. Natural and social sounds need a stable baseline.
- Track how a sound changes the protagonist's next action, rather than using it only as decoration.
